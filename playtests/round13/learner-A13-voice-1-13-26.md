## Q7 first — is this teaching me, or is it a quiz that moves on?

**It is a quiz that moves on, with a very good textbook bolted onto the side of it.**

Those are two different systems and you can hear the seam. When the app *decides* to teach me
— the grammar notes, the opening cards, the answers it gives when I ask a direct question —
it is genuinely excellent. The note in lesson 26 about the two feeling-frames, with both
exceptions, is better than anything I've been read in this course. The lesson 13 opener that
warns me the stem is unpredictable and only the ending transfers is real teaching.

But the part that reacts to **me** — the wrong-answer explainer — is not connected to that
knowledge. It is guessing. Four separate times it told me which part of my word was wrong and
named the wrong part. Once it told me to fix something that was the opposite of the actual
error and cost me a turn. Once it invented a meaning for a word it had taught me five minutes
earlier. And the checker underneath it is so lenient that I got a person-ending wrong, a case
ending wrong, and the entire `-ga` rule that lesson 26 exists to teach wrong — and was told
"Great job!", "Perfect!", "Right."

So the shape of the experience is: I make a mistake, something plausible-sounding but often
false is said at me, and the lesson advances. That is a quiz. The teaching happens *around* it,
on a schedule, regardless of what I did.

The specific thing that would flip this: **the explainer clearly does not know what word I
actually said.** Every time I pushed back with a question, the tutor immediately gave the
correct analysis. The information is in the building. It just isn't in the room where wrong
answers are handled.

## A. Wrong answers now get a spoken explanation of what YOU said

**Delivered, but unreliable to the point of being dangerous.** Something is always spoken now —
only once (#13) did I get a bare hint with no analysis. So the bare "Not quite" is mostly gone.

The problem is accuracy. Tally across 13 wrong answers:

- **2 were genuinely good** (#2, #12) — named my word or my two faults, and I fixed it myself.
- **3 were correct but withheld the point** (#4, #8, #10) — right about *where*, silent about
  *why*, when the why was one sentence away in the same card.
- **1 was a bare hint, no analysis** (#13).
- **5 named the wrong part of the word** (#1, #6, #7, #10, and #3's flattened definition) — four
  of them insisting on "the ending / last syllable" when the ending was the part I had right.
- **2 gave an instruction that was backwards or partial and cost me a turn** (#5, #9). Both
  times the *second* attempt produced the correct rule, which means the correct rule exists.
- **1 fabricated a meaning for a word from the current lesson** (#11).

The pattern I'd point an engineer at: the explainer reaches for "your ending is off" as a
default when it can't actually diff the two strings, and it never seems to check whether my
answer is itself a course word with a known meaning. #2 and #12 prove it can do the right
thing. #6, #7 and #11 are cases where my answer was a word the app taught me *in that same
lesson* and it didn't recognise it.

## B. Feedback spoken in full, no truncation

**Fixed. I could not break it.** Every note I heard ran to its end, caveats included:

- L13 opener: "…and there is **no rule** that gets you from one to the other. Learn each verb's
  stem as its own word; the ENDING is what transfers." Full.
- L1 `Bagunnanu` note: ran all the way through to "…and why the clipped *bagunna* you hear in
  fast speech still means the same thing." Full.
- **L26, the flagged one.** Spoken verbatim: *"Two frames, and both are accepted: naaku X-ga
  undi and nenu X-ga unnanu. Use either with any feeling here. **Two exceptions: denying one
  drops the -ga — naaku badha ledu — and speaking about someone else takes unnaru, as in ayana
  badhaga unnaru.**"* Both exceptions present, spoken text matches the screen word for word.
  Nothing overstated, nothing contradicted. **This is the fix working.**
- The `-ga` rule was also stated correctly in the very first line of the lesson, including
  "Denying a feeling is the exception — there the bare noun is used."

**Two truncation bugs remain, and both eat numbers:**

- L1: screen `🎓 **Vocabulary done** — 6 words.` → spoken **"Vocabulary done — words."**

Numerals are being stripped from the spoken channel. "From lesson joins the two verbs" is a

**One contradiction, but it's the checker's fault, not the speech's:** in L26 the frame note is

## C. The `skip` command

**Works. 4/4, at four different stages.** Tried it in L1 mid-vocab and mid-sentence-building,
in L13 on the last vocab card, and in L26 on a two-word card.

- Always the same clean shape: *"No problem — it's **X**. That one will come back later."* then
  straight to the next item. Never hung, never argued.
- What it gave me made sense every time. The L1 sentence skip returned `Nenu bagunnanu, meeru?`
  which taught me that "and you?" is just `meeru?` — I'd asked for exactly that a turn earlier
  and got an honest "that has not come up yet — good catch, and it is the course's gap, not
  yours. Say 'skip' and I will give you the answer." That hand-off was the best-designed moment
  in three lessons.

**Two rough edges:**

1. **Skipping the last vocab card swallows the section announcement.** In L13 and L26 I skipped
   the final word and went straight into the memory check with no "Vocabulary done — quick
   memory check next." L1, where I didn't skip that slot, announced it properly. So skip eats
   the signpost — see Q5.
2. **"That one will come back later" is sometimes false, and sometimes instantly false.** In
   L13 I skipped `Cheppanu` and the *very next thing said to me* was "What's the Telugu word for
   'I spoke / I said'?" Less than a second between "later" and later. In L26 the two-word skip
   returned "it's **Alupu Bhayam**" as one blob — those are two unrelated words ("tiredness",
   "fear") and reading them as a single answer is confusing by ear.

## The eight questions

**1. Did I understand WHY an answer was wrong, from audio alone?**

Sometimes, and I couldn't tell which times to trust. #2 and #12: yes, completely, and I fixed
them unaided. #4, #8, #10: I knew *where* but not *why*, so I was pattern-matching sounds, not
learning a rule. #1, #6, #7: I understood something confidently and it was **false** — I spent a
turn hunting for a wrong consonant that didn't exist, and a turn staring at the last syllable of
`Tintunnanu` which was correct. #11 taught me a fake word meaning.

The honest answer is that audio alone left me **less** sure than a bare "Not quite" would have,
because a confident wrong explanation is worse than no explanation. I only ever got straight by
arguing back.

**2. Did hearing an explanation let me get a LATER answer right? — Yes, three clear cases.**

- **The strongest one, and it's the flagged fix.** L26, the frame note's exception *"denying one
  drops the -ga — naaku badha ledu"*. Two prompts later: "Say 'I am not sad'." I said
  `Naaku badha ledu` → **"Spot on!"** I had never seen that written down, never been drilled on
  it, and the `-ga` habit pulled the other way. That sentence is exactly the tail that used to be
  cut off. **The fix bought me a correct answer.**
- Same note, second exception: *"speaking about someone else takes unnaru, as in ayana badhaga
  unnaru."* Final turn of L26: "Point to a sad friend: 'He is sad'." → `Ayana badhaga unnaru`,
  correct, purely by ear.
- L13: the second word-order reply ("the subject should come first, followed by 'that' and then
  the verb") gave me SOV. Next prompt, 'I ate food', unaided: `Nenu annam thinnanu` → "Spot on!"

Worth saying plainly: **all three came from the scheduled notes and the corrected second
attempts — not one came from a first wrong-answer explanation.**

**3. Verdict, explanation, next instruction — is that the order?**

Usually yes, and it's the right order. **But it inverts on the final turn of every lesson, all
three times.** L1, L13, L26: the grammar note played first, then "Correct! That's the last one."
So the last thing I do in every lesson, I sit through an explanation not knowing whether I
passed. It's the one turn where I most want the verdict first, and it's the one turn that
withholds it. L13 had a second inversion mid-lesson (`Nenu illu vellanu` → "Perfect!" then a note
about `chesaru`/`chesadu` that had nothing to do with the sentence I'd just built).

**4. Anything spoken that shouldn't be?**

- **Markdown read aloud.** Every card is `**Namaskaram**` and `*santhoshamga*`. Whether the
  asterisks reach my ear depends on the TTS, but they're in the string.
- **The paw emoji `🐾` is in the spoken line of nearly every card.** Not on the screen line —
  in the spoken one.
- **Dropped numerals** (see B): "Vocabulary done — words", "from lesson joins the two verbs".
- **Unclosed quotation marks**, three times, always in wrong-answer explanations: `the word for
  "you.` / `the phrase for "I'm fine.` / `the verb "saw.` By ear that's a sentence that just
  stops.
- **A spoken ellipsis:** *"meeru … unnaru, ayana … unnaru"* — reads as a stall.
- **Verbatim repeats used as answers to questions.** L13: I asked where a time word goes and got
  the `chesaru`/`chesadu` note I'd already heard earlier in the lesson, word for word. L26: I
  asked which syllable it meant and got the opening `-ga` note replayed in full.
- **The end-of-lesson recap is a run-on list.** "Namaskaram — Hello, Nenu — I, Meeru — You,
  Unnaru — You are, Bagunnanu — I'm fine, Ela — How" as one breath. Six pairs with no pause
  structure; by ear it's noise, and it's the one moment I'd actually want to absorb.

**5. Did I know where I was when the format changed?**

Mostly. "Vocabulary done — quick memory check next." and "Review passed — now let's build whole
sentences." and "Phrases done — now real conversation." are all spoken, and they land — I always
knew a new kind of thing was starting.

**Except when I'd skipped the last vocab card.** L13 and L26 both dropped straight from a skip
into "What's the Telugu word for…" with no announcement. Both times I genuinely didn't know
whether I was being drilled again or tested, and in L13 it was on the word I'd *just* skipped,
which made it feel like the app hadn't registered the skip. L1, no skip at that slot, announced
it cleanly. So the signpost is real and the skip path loses it.

**7. Teaching or quiz?** — at the top.

**8. After three lessons, what could I say unprompted — and by ear or by eye?**

**By ear, confidently, without looking:**

- `Namaskaram, nenu bagunnanu, meeru ela unnaru?` — the whole greeting exchange, one breath. I
  never read this; I built it from drills and the word-order correction.
- `Nenu annam thinnanu` / `Nenu adhi chusanu` — and more usefully, **the SOV rule underneath
  them**: who, what, verb last. That's from the corrected second explanation in L13.
- `Naaku badha ledu`, `Naaku bhayam ledu` — denials, **including the exception that they drop the
  `-ga`**. This is the one I'm proudest of and it exists *only* because the caveat was spoken.
- `Ayana badhaga unnaru` — third person, by ear from the same note.
- `-nu` is me, `-ru` is you, and it rides on every verb: `chesanu`/`chesaru`, `unnanu`/`unnaru`,
  `bagunnanu`/`bagunnaru`. I can hear a sentence's subject now without being told. That's the
  single most valuable thing I took, and it came from two scheduled notes plus a question I had
  to ask twice.

**By eye only:**

- The `--notes` list. Twenty-five lessons of vocabulary I can recognise on a page and could not
  produce in speech.
- That `intiki` is the "to the house" form — I only know it's a distinct word because it was
  written next to `Illu`; the app accepted my wrong `illu` out loud, so my ear learned nothing.

**Things I actively believe that are wrong, from audio:**

- That `Nanu`/`Nenu` differ in a consonant (told to me flatly; only corrected because I argued).
- That `alupu` and `alupuga` are interchangeable in "I am tired" (I was told "Perfect!" for the
  wrong one).
- That `unnanu` is acceptable when asking Miko a question ("Great job!").
- Very nearly, that `Badha` means "obstacle."

**Net:** three lessons in, I can hold a short greeting exchange and describe how I feel, and I
understand two real grammatical rules — the person-ending and the feeling-frame — well enough to
generate sentences I was never taught. That's a genuine result and the full-length notes earned
it. It sits alongside four false beliefs, all four of which came from the wrong-answer explainer
or from an uncaught error. **Fix the explainer's word-diffing and tighten the checker, and this
is a very good course.** Right now the teaching and the grading are pulling in opposite
directions, and the grading is louder because it's the part that responds to me.
