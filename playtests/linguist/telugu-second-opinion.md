## 1. "Akkada vellandi" vs "Akkadiki vellandi" (Go there)

**Answer:** Yes, it should be *Akkadiki vellandi*. **Confidence:** high.

*akkaDa* is the locative ("there, at that place"). With a verb of motion, Telugu marks the goal with the dative *-ki/-ku* (*akkaDa-ki*, *inTi-ki*, *baDi-ki*). *Akkada vellandi* sounds like a learner error. In fast speech it might pass as "go over there", but it should not be the taught form. If the grader needs leniency, treat it as a near miss rather than a full pass.

- Wikipedia, *Telugu grammar*: the dative case is *-ki/-ku*, with the example *baḍiki* = "goes **to school**". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telugu_grammar
- Wikivoyage Telugu phrasebook: "Take me there, please" = *nannu **akkadiki** teesukuvellandi*. https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Telugu

## 2. "Naaku chala snehithulu undi" vs "...unnaru" (I have many friends)

**Answer:** Yes, reject *undi* and pass only *unnaru*. **Confidence:** high.

*snehitulu* is a human plural. Telugu verbs agree in the human plural with *-aaru* (*unnaaru*). *undi* is the 3rd-person singular non-masculine form, so the agreement is wrong. The most natural phrasing is ***Naaku chaalaa mandi snehitulu unnaaru***: *mandi* is the counter for people, and *chaalaa snehitulu* without it sounds slightly bare. Consider accepting both with and without *mandi*, and also *friends unnaaru*, which is extremely common in real speech.

- Wikipedia, *Telugu grammar*: *veḷtunnāru* "they (masc./fem.) are going" vs *veḷtunnāyi* "they (neuter)". It also notes that numbers above seven use the measure word *mandi* for humans. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telugu_grammar
- Rutgers LGSA paper on Telugu person/number agreement: a plural subject with a singular predicate is ungrammatical. https://sites.rutgers.edu/lgsa/wp-content/uploads/sites/50/2021/05/Raghotham.pdf

## 3. "Adhi chedu" vs "Adhi baaledu"; "Chedu annam"

**Answer:** For "That is bad", people say ***Adhi baaledu*** (or *bAgaa lEdu*, or *adi manchidi kaadu*). *Adhi chedu* is understandable but stilted. *Chedu annam* is not something people say. **Confidence:** medium-high.

*ceDu* is real, but it is the moral/evaluative "bad, evil, vile". Its natural habitats are *ceDu alavaaTlu* (bad habits), *ceDu snEhaalu* (bad company) and *manci-ceDu* (good and evil). It is not the everyday "bad" for food, films or weather. That everyday sense is *baaledu* (contracted from *baagaa leedu*, "isn't good"). Food that has gone off is *paaDaina annam*, *cheDipOyina annam* or *paachi annam*, and food that tastes bad is *annam baaledu*. Do not teach *chedu annam*. Accepting *adhi chedu* as a pass would be lenient but defensible. It should not be the model answer.

- Gwynn, p. 193: *ceḍu* "I. n. evil. II. adj. bad, vile, worthless". The register is moral/evaluative. https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/gwynn_query.py?qs=ceDu&searchhws=yes
- Wikivoyage: *Naaku aarogyam **baaledu*** "I don't feel so great". This is *baaledu* as the everyday "not good". https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Telugu
- Evidence for "chedu annam is not said" is my own judgement as a reader of the language. No corpus check was possible.

## 4. "Bhojanam vedi" vs "Bhojanam vediga undi" (The meal is hot)

**Answer:** It must be *Bhojanam vediga undi* (or *vEDigaa undi*). **Confidence:** medium-high.

Telugu does have verbless equational sentences (*idi pustakam*, "this is a book"). But a state or quality predicated of a noun normally takes *-gaa undi*: *vEDigaa undi*, *challagaa undi*, *baagaa undi*. *Bhojanam vedi* reads like a label or a telegraphic fragment ("meal, hot"). Accept *annam vEDigaa undi* too, since that is how people actually say it about food. *bhOjanam* is a little formal for everyday speech.

- Gwynn, p. ~1180: *vēḍi* "I. n. heat, warmth; mild fever. II. adj. hot, warm". Its adjective use is attributive (*vEDi niiLLu*, hot water), which supports the need for *-gaa undi* in predicate position. https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/gwynn_query.py?qs=వేడి&searchhws=yes
- The *-gaa undi* rule is from my own knowledge. I found no web page that states it.

## 5. "maa" vs "naa" for family and home

**Answer:** Both are acceptable. ***maa* should be the default**, and *naa* is less idiomatic, not wrong. **Confidence:** high.

Speakers say *maa amma*, *maa nanna*, *maa illu*, *maa ooru* even when talking about themselves alone, because family, home and hometown are thought of as shared. *naa amma* or *naa illu* is grammatical. It sounds emphatic or possessive, or slightly translated-from-English. *naa* remains normal for strictly personal things (*naa pen*, *naa phone*, *naa peru*).

- Gwynn, entry *maa*: "1 our (excluding the person[s] addressed). 2 maa is used for naa in speaking of s.g which is or can be shared with others: **maa illu** my house; **maa tammuDu** my younger brother; maa waaru my husband; maa aawiDa my wife." https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/gwynn_query.py?qs=మా&searchhws=yes
- This holds in both Telangana and coastal Andhra.

## 6. "Naaku pusthakam undi" vs "Naa daggara pusthakam undi"

**Answer:** Both are acceptable, with a nuance. **Confidence:** high.

Dative + *undi* is the standard Telugu "have" (own/possess: *naaku illu undi*, *naaku car undi*). *naa daggara* (lit. "near me") means "on me / with me right now". In everyday speech it is the more natural choice for small portable things you are carrying (*naa daggara pen undi*, *naa daggara Dabbulu leevu*). For "I have a book" both pass. Do not reject *Naaku pusthakam undi*.

- Gwynn, p. 255, *daggira/daggara*, sense 2, "with, on (one's person)": *ippuDu **naa daggara** Dabbu leedu*, "I have no money on me now". https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/gwynn_query.py?qs=దగ్గిర&searchhws=yes
- UrbanPro Telugu Q&A: "Naaku pustakam undi" = "I have a book". https://www.urbanpro.com/telugu-language/what-is-the-difference-between-naaku-and

## 7. "Melukonu" vs "lev/lechi" (Wake up)

**Answer:** *Melukonu* is bookish/formal compared with *lechu*. **Confidence:** medium.

The everyday way to say "wake up!" to someone is ***lE*** / ***lEvu*** / ***nidra lE***, and "I woke up" is *lEchaanu* (*poddunne lEchaanu*). *mElukonu/mElkonu* is real and correct. It is heard mainly in formal, devotional or literary registers, and in the figurative sense "be awake / be alert". Its imperative (*mElkO*) turns up in songs and slogans more than at home. Recommendation: teach *lE/lEchi* and accept *melko/melukonu* as correct.

- Gwynn, p. 438: *meelukonu, meelkonu* v.i. "1 to wake up, rise. 2 to be awake, stay awake". It carries **no register label**, so this source does not mark it as literary. https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/gwynn_query.py?qs=mElukonu&searchhws=yes
- Gwynn: *lēcu* "to rise, get up, stand up". It does not list "wake" explicitly. https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/gwynn_query.py?qs=లేచు&searchhws=yes
- **Caveat:** the dictionaries do not settle the register question. The "bookish" judgement is mine, so confidence is medium.

## 8. "Vana paduthundi" vs "padutondi" (It is raining)

**Answer:** In the standard (coastal) system, *padutondi* = "is raining" and *padutundi* = "rains / will rain". Accept *padutundi* as a variant all the same. **Confidence:** medium.

The standard paradigm keeps the 3sg.n progressive *-tondi/-tunnadi* apart from the non-past (habitual/future) *-tundi*. "It's raining (now)" is ***vaana paDutOndi*** (coastal), and Telangana speakers say ***vaana paDutunnadi / paDtaandi***. *paDutundi* is the natural form for "it will rain" (*rEpu vaana paDutundi*) or "it rains (here in July)". In casual speech, especially in Telangana, *paDtundi* is also heard for ongoing rain, so a hard reject would be harsh. Make *padutondi* the model, and pass or soft-pass *padutundi*. Also consider accepting *padutunnadi*.

- Wikipedia, *Telugu grammar*: present tense *veḷtōndi* "She is going" vs future *veḷtundi* "She will go". https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telugu_grammar
- The Telangana variants are from my own knowledge. No source was found, so the leniency suggestion is a judgement call.

## 9. "Erupu batta" vs "erra batta" (red cloth)

**Answer:** Use ***erra batta*** (*erra guDDa*). *Erupu batta* is unidiomatic. **Confidence:** high.

*erupu* is the noun "redness / the colour red" (*adi erupu*, "it's red"; *erupu rangu*, "red colour"). Before a noun, Telugu uses the adjectival forms *erra*, *tella*, *nalla*, *paccha*. A native speaker would understand *erupu batta*, but it sounds wrong. *erupu rangu batta* ("red-coloured cloth") is acceptable if you want to keep *erupu*.

- Wiktionary *ఎర్ర*: adjective "red", described as the "adjectival form of ఎరుపు (erupu)". https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/ఎర్ర
- Gwynn, p. 90: *erupu* n. "redness". Gwynn: *erra* adj. "red, reddish brown; ~ kaLLu red eyes". https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/gwynn_query.py?qs=erra&searchhws=yes

## 10. Romanisation of వద్దు: "Vaddu", with "oddu" accepted

**Answer:** Yes. Romanise it as ***vaddu*** and accept ***oddu*** (also *voddu*, *vuddu*). **Confidence:** medium-high.

*vaddu* is the standard spelling and the one dictionaries use. In speech the initial *v* before *a* is a labial glide, and the vowel is often rounded, giving *[wɔddu]*. Many speakers write this as *voddu* or *oddu* in casual romanised chat. Any learner who learned the word by ear will type it that way.

- Wiktionary *వద్దు*: romanised *vaddu*, /ʋad̪ːu/. https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/వద్దు
- Gwynn, p. 472, writes it **"waddu"**, which shows the glide pronunciation. https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/gwynn_query.py?qs=vaddu&searchhws=yes
- Wikivoyage lists "**Vaddu/Vuddu**" as variants. https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Telugu
- **Caveat:** I found no printed source spelling it "oddu". Accepting it rests on the glide/rounding evidence above and on common chat spelling.
