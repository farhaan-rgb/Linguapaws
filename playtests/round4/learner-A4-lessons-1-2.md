I came in knowing zero Telugu. Two lessons, 15 steps each, no crashes, no loops. Overall: this was a genuinely decent experience and a big step up from what I'd been warned about. The teaching is clear, the grammar notes are the best part, and — the big one — **every single question I asked got a real answer.** Six questions, six answers. Nothing vanished, nothing got the "I'll come back to that" brush-off.

## What I actually learned and could use unprompted

After lesson 1 I could greet someone and do the whole exchange without looking anything up: `Namaskaram, nenu bagunnanu, meeru ela unnaru?` I produced that cold, in one go, on the last step. I also understood *why* it works — that `bagunnanu` is `baga` + `unnanu` glued together, so there's no separate word for "am", and that the `-nu` / `-ru` ending is what tells you who you're talking about. That's a real piece of grammar I'd carry into lesson 3.

After lesson 2 I could point at things: `Idhi emiti?` / `Adhi emiti?` / `Idhi pusthakam` / `Ee pusthakam`. And I genuinely understand the `Idhi` vs `Ee` split now, which I gather was the thing that fell over last time. See Q2 below.

Twelve words, all six from each lesson stuck.

That's it. It stops mid-sentence with a stray backslash. So I was marked *not quite right* and then never told what was right — the sentence that was supposed to tell me got cut off. The grammar note that followed then said my answer *was* the natural form:

> 💡 ... so *Nenu bagunnanu, meeru?* is the natural form here

So I had it exactly right, got told "very close", and got a broken correction. That's the one place I'd have been annoyed if I were paying for this.

**Being told "It is spelled X" when I hadn't misspelled anything.** Twice:

> You: Namaskaram, Bagunnanu
> Tutor: Perfect! It is spelled **Namaskaram, nenu bagunnanu**.

> You: Ela unnaru?
> Tutor: Correct! It is spelled **Meeru ela unnaru**.

Both of mine were spelled correctly. The tutor was actually adding an optional word (`nenu`, `meeru`), not fixing a spelling. As a beginner my first reaction was "wait, did I get it wrong?" — I stopped and asked about it, which is a turn I wouldn't have needed to spend.

**Two words dumped in one turn.** Twice — `Bagunnanu` + `Ela` together, and `Pusthakam` + `Peru` together — with both pronunciations crammed into one hint bracket:

> [say it like: Bagunnanu — baa-gun-naa-nu, Ela — eh-laa]

I only got asked to repeat one of the two. The other one I was just expected to have absorbed, and it turned up in the memory check straight after.

**`{w}` in the middle of the most important explanation in lesson 2** (details below). Not fatal because the rest of the sentence carried the meaning, but it's right in the paragraph that defines `Idhi` vs `Ee`.

**Replying "ok" gets marked wrong.** I tried it twice out of laziness. Both times: "Not quite!" and the same prompt again. Fair enough, but "ok" isn't a wrong answer, it's not an answer — a nudge would land better than a red X.

## 2. Did any explanation contradict the lesson? (`Idhi` vs `Ee`)

**No. This is fixed, and it was told to me consistently three separate times.**

The lesson's own teaching:

> *Idhi* stands alone, as the whole subject of a sentence; {w} leans on the noun that follows it — *ee illu* is "this house", not "this is a house". *Adhi* does the same, becoming *aa*. **It is not about distance**: near or far is *idhi* against *adhi*, while *idhi* against *ee* is standing alone against sitting in front of a noun.

I then asked directly, mid-lesson, and got:

> Idhi means "this" when standing alone, while Ee is used before a noun, like in "ee peru" for "this name". **It's not about distance, but how you use it!**

And the note after the `Ee pusthakam` drill:

> Two words, no verb — *ee pusthakam* names a thing, it does not say anything about it yet. Put *idhi* in front instead and you get a whole sentence: *idhi pusthakam*, "this is a book". That is the difference the two forms carry, and **it is not about distance**.

No distance claim anywhere, no collision with `Adhi` (which is separately and correctly taught as "something further away"). I understood it and used both forms correctly on the first attempt.
