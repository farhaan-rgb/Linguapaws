# Learner E4 — Telugu lessons 22 & 26

Beginner learner, English only, notebook = lessons 1–21 (and 22–25 by the time I started 26).
Both lessons finished, 15/15 steps each, 14 turns apiece. No loops, no repeated-reply deadlock.

---

## How it actually went

Honestly? Better than I expected on the teaching, worse than I expected on being listened to.

**Lesson 22 (can / can't)** was the cleanest thing this course has given me so far. The very first
line told me `-galanu` = "I can" and `-lenu` = "I can't" are *endings*, and then a tip later spelled
out the thing I most needed to hear: the stem changes shape before them (`cheya-`, not `cheyu-`), so
learn them as pairs and don't try to build new ones yourself. That's an honest, useful warning. It
stopped me inventing rubbish.

The moment I'm actually proud of: asked for "I can help", I had `Sahaayam` as a noun and nothing
that looked like a verb "to help". I guessed `Sahaayam cheyagalanu` — "I can do help" — said out
loud I was guessing, and it was right. Then the tip told me *why* ("Telugu has no single verb 'to
help'") and handed me a second sentence I could build from words I already had (`pani cheyagalanu`).
That is teaching. I came out of lesson 22 able to say "I can do X" for any noun in my notebook,
unprompted.

**Lesson 26 (feelings)** front-loaded the `-ga` rule properly — line one, before anything was
graded: *"The feeling words in this lesson are NOUNS, and to say you feel one you add -ga:
santhoshamga. Denying a feeling is the exception — there the bare noun is used."* Both halves of
that turned out to be true and both were tested. Big improvement.

**Where I got stuck, and it was the same wall twice.** The whole difficulty of lesson 26 is *which
frame* a feeling goes in — `Nenu X-ga unnanu` or `Naaku X-ga undi`. I asked that question, in plain
English, twice, before I was graded on it. Both times the tutor said "Good" / "Exactly!" and moved
straight to the next word without a syllable of answer. So when the drill came I had to guess, and I
said so in my answer.

That guess was accepted — and *then* I got the rule, and the rule is genuinely good: *"Use naaku X-ga
undi ... and you will be right with every feeling in this lesson."* That is an actionable default I
can carry to a new word. But I had to gamble a graded answer to buy it, and the two turns where I
politely asked for exactly that were thrown away.

**Two things that confused me and still do:**

1. The rule says `naaku ... undi` is the reliable default for feelings. Four turns later "He is sad"
   turns out to be `Ayana badhaga unnaru` — the *other* frame. So the default silently isn't the
   default once the subject stops being me. Nobody flagged that.
2. `Nenu santhoshamga unnanu` for "I am happy" was accepted, which matches the tip's aside about
   `santhosham` being the exception. Fine — but that means the "reliable default" already has a
   named exception inside the same lesson.

**What felt bad:** being told "It is spelled **Nenu cheyagalanu**" one line after a tip told me
`cheyagalanu` alone is complete and `nenu` is optional. I hadn't misspelled anything. It happened
five times across the two lessons and every time it made me doubt a correct answer.

## Can I build a form myself?

**After lesson 22 — "I can't speak":** Yes, and I'd have got it right without help. `Matladagalanu`
is "I can speak"; the pair-swap I was explicitly taught is `-galanu` → `-lenu`; so `Matladalenu`.
I'm flagging that as reasoning-not-repetition: nobody showed me that word. I'm ~85% confident, and
the only reason it isn't 100% is the lesson's own warning that stems reshape.

**After lesson 26 — "I am angry":** Yes — `Naaku kopamga undi`, and I know *why*: `kopam` is a noun,
`-ga` makes it the felt version, and `naaku ... undi` is the stated default for feelings. I could
also produce `Nenu kopamga unnanu` and know it's the weight-on-me variant.

**Would I know which frame for a genuinely NEW feeling word?** Now, yes — default to
`naaku X-ga undi`. But *only* because of the tip that arrived after I'd already been graded, and
only for "I". For "he/she" I'd now reach for `Ayana X-ga unnaru`, purely by copying the one example,
not by any rule I was given.

### 2. Is the bare verb accepted?

**Yes — the previous tester's contradiction did not recur.** I tested the exact case.

  It is spelled **Eeroju nenu cheyalenu**."* **Accepted.**

Residual bug: acceptance is announced as a *spelling correction*. Every bare answer came back as

### 3. Is the `-ga` frame taught before you're graded on it?

**The `-ga` suffix: yes, comprehensively, in the very first line** — *"The feeling words in this
lesson are NOUNS, and to say you feel one you add -ga: santhoshamga. Denying a feeling is the

**The choice between the two sentence frames: no.** Nothing about `naaku ... undi` vs

### 4. Is there an actionable rule for choosing the frame?

**Yes, finally, and it's a real one.** No more "fixed by the sentence, not the feeling":

> *"Two frames, and a default you can rely on. Use **naaku** X-ga **undi** ... and you will be right
> with every feeling in this lesson. **Nenu** X-ga **unnanu** ... is the same meaning with the weight
> on you, and it is the usual choice with santhosham; both are accepted here. Denying a feeling is
> the one that differs: naaku badha ledu, bare noun, no -ga."*

I could apply that to a new feeling word tomorrow. Two dents in it: it arrives only after a graded
guess (see Q3), and it's quietly wrong for third person — "He is sad" turned out to be
`Ayana badhaga unnaru`, the *other* frame, with no acknowledgement that the "reliable default" had
just stopped applying.

The tutor's own conversational answer was also waffle compared to the tip: *"You should use 'Naaku
alupuga undi' ... when you want to emphasize that it is to you. The other form ... is also correct...
Both are acceptable, but the first one aligns with the structure for feelings."* If the tip hadn't
followed, I'd have learned nothing from that.

### 5. "I can help" — clear how to build it?

**Yes — best-designed step in either lesson.** I derived `Sahaayam cheyagalanu` unaided from
`Sahaayam` (noun) + `Cheyagalanu`, and the tip then confirmed the mechanism and generalised it:
*"Telugu has no single verb 'to help'. It says 'do help' ... The same join builds pani cheyagalanu,
'I can do work', out of words you already have."* I can now do "I can do <any noun>" cold.

### 6. "He is sad" — was a third-person "is" available?

**Not before the question; yes after it, and the explanation was good.** I had no third-person copula
at all and said so. Reply: `Ayana badhaga unnaru. (He is sad.)` plus a genuinely illuminating tip:
*"Unnaru is doing a second job here. You learned it as 'you are', and it is also the respectful 'he
is / she is / they are' ... So the frame does not change, only the person in front of it."* It even
volunteered `Ame badhaga unnaru` for "she is sad".

So: unbuildable in advance, well recovered on request. Nothing marks the double duty of `unnaru`
anywhere in my notebook — L1 has it as "You are" only — so a learner who guesses instead of asking
is stuck. And as noted, this contradicts the `naaku` default without saying so.
