# Kannada course playtest — lessons 1–10, then exam from memory

Learner profile: adult native English speaker, zero prior exposure to Kannada or any Indian language.
Sessions K3full1 … K3full10, scenarios 1–10 in order.

---

## Section 1 — The play-through

### Lesson 1 — Greetings (Namaskara, Naanu, Neevu, Chennagiddeeni, Hege, Iddeera)

Smooth start. Six words, all drilled one at a time with a pronunciation respelling, which I found genuinely
useful — `nuh-mus-kaa-ruh` is the only reason I have any idea what *Namaskara* sounds like.

The teaching note after the first sentence was the best thing in the lesson:

> *Chennagiddeeni* is a whole sentence on its own — the -eeni on the end IS the "I" … The matching "you"
> ending is the -eera you met in *iddeera*. That is the whole system: change the ending, change who you mean.

That reframed six vocabulary items into one rule, and I've leaned on it since.

**Asked but not taught.** "Say 'I am fine, and you?'" — I had no word for "and", said so explicitly:

> I don't know the word for 'and' — you haven't taught it. Best I can do is: Chennagiddeeni. Neevu?

The tutor's response was unhelpful — *"That is the note just above — have another look"* — pointing me at a
note that did not in fact contain the answer, then re-asking. When I gave `Chennagiddeeni, neevu?` it was
accepted and only THEN did the explanation appear: no "and" is needed, *matte* exists but a bare *neevu?* is
how it's really said. **That note should come before the exercise, not after.** Being told "look at the note
above" when the note above doesn't cover it is the one moment in ten lessons where I felt actively misled.

Also noted: my flagging of a gap got parsed as a wrong answer rather than as a question. The tutor never
acknowledged that I'd said "I don't know this word".

### Lesson 2 — This / that / what (Idu, Adu, Yenu, Pustaka, Hesaru)

Very clean. The i-/a- near-far contrast was taught explicitly *at the point of introduction*:

> *idu* and **Adu** differ by one vowel at the very front … Get it wrong and you have said the opposite.

I guessed `Idu yenu?` for "what is this?" purely from "the pointing word probably comes first" — and got the
rule confirmed afterwards: pointing word first, question word LAST. Good ordering: guess, then rule.

The note on the missing copula was excellent and pre-empted exactly the confusion I was about to have:

> There is no word for "is" in this sentence, and none is missing … You will meet *ide* ("it is") next
> lesson, for saying where something is; here it would be wrong.

Final item was "Ask 'What is the name?'" — I gave `Hesaru yenu?` and was told it was correct, with a note
that "your name" needs *nimma* from lesson 5. Honest sign-posting, no complaint.

### Lesson 3 — Location (Yelli, Mane, Illi, Alli, Ide, Hoguttene, Hogu)

The heaviest lesson so far — seven items — but they hang together. The i-/a- pattern got its second outing
(*illi*/*alli*) and the app explicitly linked it back to *idu*/*adu*, which is what made it stick.

Two subtle things taught well:
- *Ide* is for THINGS. People in a place usually take no verb at all: *naanu illi*. I'd have got this wrong
  without the note, and the drill "Someone asks 'Neevu yelli?'" tested exactly that.
- The `-ge` destination ending was introduced attached to *mane* → *manege* at first presentation, so when the
  "I am going home" drill came I already had it. I answered `Naanu manege hoguttene` first try.

The contrast note at the end is the single clearest thing in the course:

> *idu pustaka* needs no "is" at all, because that sentence says what a thing IS, not where it is.

No confusion in this lesson.

### Lesson 4 — Want / don't want (Beku, Beda, Oota, Neeru, Haudu, Illa, Dhanyavada)

**My own error to flag honestly:** for "I want water" I typed `Nanage neeru beku` — and *nanage* had NOT been
taught at that point. I pattern-guessed a dative form off the `-ge` ending from lesson 3 and it happened to
land. The app accepted it and the note then confirmed *nanage neeru beku* as the explicit form. That is luck,
not learning, and I've excluded *nanage* from what I count as "known" in the exam below.

The *beku*/*beda* framing was good — *beda* replaces *beku*, it is not "no" + "want":

> *Beda* is not the word "no" — it is a whole refusal … Same shape, opposite meaning: *oota beku* / *oota beda*.

The *illa* vs *illi* warning was placed right at *illa*'s introduction ("Mind the last letter"). Appreciated —
those two are genuinely one letter apart and I would have merged them.

Slight gap: I was told *illa* "goes at the end when it negates a sentence" but never got to drill that. It
stayed theory.

### Lesson 5 — Possessives (Nanna, Nimma, Aatana, Avara, Kathe)

Mechanically easy — possessive in front, noun unchanged, same as English.

**The one badly-worded prompt in the whole course.** "Point at a book across the room and tell Miko that the
book is HERS." I answered `Adu avara pustaka` and was marked wrong:

> Not quite. avara means "His / her (respectful)" — here you want the word for "Your".

The prompt says "hers" and the answer is "your" — because "her" refers to Miko, the person I'm speaking to.
That is a trap in the *English*, not a test of Kannada. The clarifying note exists and is good ("Watch which
'your' an English prompt means") but it only fires AFTER you've been marked wrong. **Capitalising HERS makes
it worse — it reads as emphasis on the possessor's gender, which points you straight at *avara*.**

**A parser bug.** When I retried I wrote my reasoning inline: `Oh, I see - I'm talking to her, so it's 'your'.
Adu nimma pustaka`. The correct Kannada was in there verbatim, but I got:

> Not quite. You missed the word for "that" at the beginning of your sentence.

— which is factually wrong, *Adu* was present. The English preamble broke the check. Bare `Adu nimma pustaka`
passed immediately. So: **the answer checker cannot cope with a learner thinking out loud**, and its error
message in that case is confidently incorrect rather than "I couldn't parse that".

*Kathe* ("story") was taught and reviewed but never used in a single sentence beyond `Aatana kathe`. It feels
like filler.

### Lesson 6 — People (Yaaru, Ivaru, Snehita, Shikshaka, Akka, Avaru)

Third outing of the i-/a- pattern (*ivaru*/*avaru*) and by now I was predicting it rather than learning it,
which the app said out loud:

> Once you have noticed the i-/a- pattern you can predict it rather than memorise it.

That is the course's best structural decision — one pattern, three lessons, escalating.

The *ivaru* = "he/she when present" note was necessary; English "she" has no direct equivalent here and I'd
have hunted for one.

Small friction: *Snehita* is taught as "friend" here and *shikshaka* as "teacher", both singular, and neither
gets a plural until lesson 8 — which is fine, but "Introduce your friend to Miko" and "this is my teacher"
both accepted `Ivaru nanna X` with no gender or number decision at all, so the drills were easier than they
looked.

### Lesson 7 — Numbers 1–5 (Ondu, Eradu, Mooru, Naalku, Aidu)

Easiest lesson. Five items, no grammar except one genuinely important rule:

> The noun does NOT change after a number — *eradu pustaka*, not a plural form.

I guessed `Eradu pustaka` on instinct (numeral + bare noun felt right) and got the rule as confirmation.

Nice touch: the last drill, "Ask for two waters", forced lessons 4 + 7 together (`Eradu neeru beku`). That is
the first time the course made me *combine* rather than *recall*.

### Lesson 8 — Plurals (Galu, Pustakagalu, Manegalu, Hoovu, Hoovugalu, Snehitaru)

The people/things split (`-aru` vs `-galu`) is taught head-on with the counterexample stated before I could
get it wrong:

> "Friends" is **Snehitaru** — and NOT *snehitagalu*. People take -aru; things take -galu.

And the closing note is the one that made lesson 7 and 8 lock together:

> *eradu pustaka* ("two books", no ending, because the number said it) and *nanna pustakagalu* ("my books",
> ending needed, because nothing else did).

That is exactly the question I had forming, answered before I asked it. Best-sequenced note in the course.

Teaching `Galu` as a standalone "word" in the vocabulary drill is odd — I was asked to *say* an ending on its
own, and the app itself admits "It is not a word you say on its own". It also then appears in the "You can now
say" summary as a word. Minor, but it's incoherent.

### Lesson 9 — Adjectives (Dodda, Chikka, Olleya, Ketta, Bisi, Chennagide)

The most conceptually valuable lesson, and it knows it:

> In front of a noun, use *olleya* … To say the food IS good, that word will not do it; you need *chennagide*.
> English uses "good" for both jobs and Kannada does not, which is the single thing to take away from this lesson.

Both attributive-only adjectives (*olleya*, *ketta*) were flagged as attributive-only at introduction, not
after a failure. That is the right way round, and is exactly what lesson 1 got wrong.

Two forms were mentioned but never drilled: *chennagilla* ("is not good", = "is bad") and *chikkadu* ("is
small"). Both arrived in passing notes on the last two items. I can recognise them; I have not practised them
and would be slow to produce them.

Also: *bisi* ("hot") is taught but there is no "cold". For water, that is a fifty-percent gap in the most
common thing you'd ask for.

### Lesson 10 — Shop / restaurant (Kshamisi, Lekka, Kodi, Eshtu, Sari)

Good closer, almost entirely recombination. `Namaskara, eradu neeru kodi` pulled lessons 1, 4, 7 and 10 into
one line and the app said so.

The politeness rule arrived here and retroactively fixed lesson 3:

> the blunt *hogu* ("go!") from lesson 3 becomes the polite *hogi*, exactly as *kodu* becomes *kodi*.

That is useful — but it means lesson 3 taught me to say "go there" in a form I'd now hesitate to use on a
stranger, and nothing in lesson 3 warned me. *Hogi* is never drilled.

**Overall confusions I never got resolved:**
- No word for "and" (*matte* mentioned once, never taught or drilled).
- *Nanage* was accepted from me before it was taught; I still don't know its shape well enough to reuse.
- The two-form split (attributive vs predicate) is taught for *olleya*/*chennagide* but the predicate forms
  *chennagilla* and *chikkadu* are only mentioned, never practised.
- No yes/no question marker anywhere, so I can ask "who/what/where/how much" but cannot ask "is this…?".
- No past tense, no plain 3rd-person pronoun, no verbs besides "go", "want", "give", "be".

## Section 2 — The exam (from memory, no app)

### Part 1 — Say it in Kannada

1. **Hello, I am fine.** — `Namaskara, naanu chennagiddeeni.` (Drilled verbatim. *Naanu* is optional — the
   -eeni ending already says "I" — so `Namaskara, chennagiddeeni` is equally correct.)
2. **What is this?** — `Idu yenu?` (Drilled.)
3. **Where is the house?** — `Mane yelli ide?` (Drilled.)
4. **I want water.** — `Neeru beku.` (Drilled. The explicit form is *nanage neeru beku*, but *nanage* was
   never taught to me — I produced it once by guesswork and the app confirmed it, so I'm not counting it as
   mine.)
5. **What is your name?** — `Nimma hesaru yenu?` (Drilled.)
6. **Two books** — `Eradu pustaka.` (Drilled. Deliberately NOT *pustakagalu*: the number already marks plural,
   so the noun stays bare.)
7. **My friends** — `Nanna snehitaru.` (Drilled. -aru not -galu, because friends are people.)
8. **The food is good.** — `Oota chennagide.` (Drilled. *Olleya* cannot end a sentence.)
9. **Ask for the bill.** — `Lekka kodi.` (Drilled.)
10. **Where is my book?** — `Nanna pustaka yelli ide?` **Assembled**, not drilled. Taking `Mane yelli ide?`
    (lesson 3) and slotting the possessive in front of the noun, which is what lesson 5 showed with
    `Nimma hesaru yenu?` — the possessive goes in and nothing else moves.
11. **I don't want hot water.** — `Bisi neeru beda.` **Assembled.** *Bisi neeru* was drilled in lesson 9;
    *beda* attaches at the end exactly as in the drilled `Ketta oota beda`.
12. **Who is that person, further away?** — `Avaru yaaru?` (Drilled — this was the exact "someone across the
    room" item.)
13. **Three flowers** — `Mooru hoovu.` **Assembled**, and I want to flag my reasoning because it's the one
    place two rules could collide. Lesson 8 gives *hoovugalu* for "flowers", but lesson 7's rule says the noun
    does not change after a number, and lesson 8 confirmed the contrast explicitly (*eradu pustaka* vs *nanna
    pustakagalu*). Number wins, so bare *hoovu*.
14. **Excuse me, where is the food?** — `Kshamisi, oota yelli ide?` **Assembled** from the drilled
    `Kshamisi, mane yelli ide?` with *oota* swapped in for *mane*.
15. **My small house** — `Nanna chikka mane.` (Drilled — possessive, then adjective, then noun.)
16. **I don't want bad food.** — `Ketta oota beda.` (Drilled.)
17. **Her name (respectfully)** — `Avara hesaru.` (Drilled. *Avara* covers respectful his AND her — no gender
    choice to make.)
18. **I am going home.** — `Naanu manege hoguttene.` (Drilled. *Naanu* optional; the -ttene ending says "I".
    The -ge on *mane* is what makes it "to the house".)

### Part 2 — What does this mean?

1. **Mane alli ide.** — "The house is there." (*Ide* is what turns "house there" into a statement that it IS
   somewhere.)
2. **Naanu manege hoguttene.** — "I am going home / I am going to the house."
3. **Ivaru nanna shikshaka.** — "This is my teacher." Literally "this person (polite) — my teacher"; no word
   for "is" is needed or missing.
4. **Adu chennagide.** — "That is good." (Not "that good thing" — *chennagide* is the predicate form.)
5. **Avaru yaaru?** — "Who is that person?" (The far one — someone across the room, not beside me.)
6. **Ketta oota beda.** — "I don't want bad food." (Literally "bad food — not wanted"; *beda* leaves the
   person to context.)

### Part 3 — Explain the rule

1. **`illi` vs `alli`, and is it a wider pattern?**
   *Illi* is "here" (near me), *alli* is "there" (further off). They differ only in the first vowel. And yes —
   this is the course's biggest structural pattern, taught three times on three different word pairs:
   `idu`/`adu` (this/that, lesson 2), `illi`/`alli` (here/there, lesson 3), `ivaru`/`avaru` (this person/that
   person, lesson 6). Initial **i- = near, initial a- = far**, consistently. Once you spot it you can predict
   the far form from the near one instead of memorising both. The practical warning that came with it: one
   vowel is the whole difference, so getting it wrong doesn't garble your sentence, it says the opposite of
   what you meant. Separate trap: *illa* ("no/not") is NOT part of this pattern despite looking like *illi* —
   it's the last letter that differs there.

2. **Why `snehitaru` and not `snehitagalu`?**
   Because Kannada uses two different plural endings depending on what you're pluralising. **People take
   `-aru`; things take `-galu`.** *Pustaka* → *pustakagalu*, *mane* → *manegalu*, *hoovu* → *hoovugalu* — all
   things. *Snehita* is a person, so it's *snehitaru*. The test the app gave for when you're unsure: ask
   whether it's a person.

3. **When `olleya`, when `chennagide`?**
   Position, not meaning. **`Olleya` only ever goes in FRONT of a noun** — *olleya oota*, "good food". It
   cannot end a sentence. **`Chennagide` is the predicate form** — it's how you say something IS good:
   *oota chennagide*, "the food is good". English uses one word "good" for both jobs; Kannada does not, and
   swapping them is wrong in both directions. Same split applies to *ketta* (attributive "bad") and to
   *chikka* vs *chikkadu* ("small" before a noun vs "is small"). Structurally, *chennagide* is *chennagi* +
   *ide* — the same "it is" from lesson 3, and the same *chennagi* hiding inside *chennagiddeeni*.

4. **What does `-ge` on `manege` do?**
   It marks the **destination** — "to the house". Kannada attaches it to the end of the noun rather than
   using a separate preposition in front, so there's no word for "to"; the noun itself changes. *Mane*
   (house) → *manege* (to the house), which is why "I am going home" is *manege hoguttene*.

5. **Is `naanu` required in `naanu chennagiddeeni`?**
   No — it's optional, and most people drop it. The **-eeni** ending on the verb already says "I", so the
   pronoun is redundant; Kannada leaves out a pronoun whenever the verb ending has already identified the
   person, which is most of the time. The one place it earns its keep is contrast — *chennagiddeeni, neevu?*
   ("I'm fine, and you?"), where you're deliberately setting yourself beside the other person.

6. **How do you say "the food is bad"?**
   `Oota chennagilla.` **Reasoning:** *ketta* is attributive-only — it can only sit in front of a noun
   (*ketta oota*, "bad food"), so it cannot be used to say the food IS bad. Kannada instead mostly negates
   the positive: *chennagilla* is *chennagi* + *illa* ("not"), i.e. "is not good". So "the food is bad" comes
   out as "the food is not good". I'm confident in the form because the app stated it twice, but I should
   flag that I was never made to produce *chennagilla* in a drill — I've only ever read it.

### Part 4 — The honesty check

1. **I ate rice yesterday.** — **I DON'T KNOW.** Three separate gaps: no word for "rice" (I have *oota*,
   "food", but that's not the same word), no word for "yesterday", and **no past tense at all** — the only
   verb forms I've been given are present (*hoguttene*) and imperative (*hogu*/*hogi*).
2. **I don't understand.** — **I DON'T KNOW.** No verb for "understand" was ever taught, and no negative
   verb form either. I know *illa* means "not" and goes at the end of a negated sentence, but with no verb to
   attach it to that gets me nowhere.
3. **Please speak slowly.** — **I DON'T KNOW.** No verb for "speak" and no word for "slowly". I do have the
   politeness mechanism — a command softens by changing its last vowel, *kodu*→*kodi*, *hogu*→*hogi* — so if
   I had the verb I could make it polite. I don't have the verb.
4. **He came to my house.** — **I DON'T KNOW.** I can build "to my house" — *nanna manege*, possessive plus
   the -ge destination ending — but I have no past tense and no verb for "come". I also have no plain "he":
   *ivaru*/*avaru* are "this/that person", used politely for he or she, and *aatana* is only the possessive
   "his". So the destination is the only part I could produce.
5. **Nine** — **I DON'T KNOW.** The course taught one to five only (*ondu, eradu, mooru, naalku, aidu*) and
   nothing above it.
6. **Where is the toilet?** — **I DON'T KNOW.** I have the whole sentence frame — `X yelli ide?` — and could
   drop any noun into it instantly. I just have no word for "toilet". This is a one-word gap in an otherwise
   complete sentence, and it's the single most useful word the course omitted.
7. **I am a teacher.** — `Naanu shikshaka.` **Answerable, assembled.** *Shikshaka* is from lesson 6, and
   lesson 2 established that a sentence saying what something IS needs no verb at all — *idu pustaka*, "this
   is a book". So pronoun plus noun, nothing between them. Mild uncertainty: every drilled example of that
   pattern used *idu*/*ivaru* rather than *naanu*, so I'm extending the rule rather than recalling it.
8. **Do you speak English?** — **I DON'T KNOW.** Three gaps: no word for "English", no verb for "speak", and
   — the structural one — **the course never taught a yes/no question**. Every question I can form uses a
   question word (*yenu, yelli, yaaru, eshtu, hege*) sitting at the end. I have no way to turn a statement
   into a question, so even with the vocabulary I could not build this.

## Section 3 — Honest self-assessment

**What I can produce unprompted, cold:** roughly fifteen fixed sentences and the ability to swap nouns inside
about six frames. Concretely — greet someone and answer "how are you"; point at something and ask what it is
or who someone is; ask where any noun I know is; say I want or don't want any noun I know; give my name and
ask for theirs; count to five and attach a number to a noun; describe something as big/small/good/bad/hot;
say the food is good; ask for the bill; ask how much something costs. That is a genuinely usable set for a
shop or a restaurant, and I'd trust myself on all of it.

**The frames matter more than the words.** `X yelli ide?`, `X beku` / `X beda`, `Idu/Adu yenu?`,
`Ivaru/Avaru yaaru?`, `X eshtu?`, `nanna/nimma + X`, `adjective + noun`. Those seven patterns are solid and
they compose — I built about half of Part 1 by slotting known nouns into them rather than recalling drills,
and I'm fairly confident in those constructions. The i-/a- near/far rule and the -aru/-galu people/things
split are the two bits of actual grammar I'd bet money on, because both were taught three times across
different lessons rather than once.

**Could I survive a five-minute exchange? No — I could survive about forty seconds of it.** I can open a
conversation and I can transact. What kills me is the second turn: I cannot say "I don't understand", I
cannot say "please repeat that", I cannot say "slowly", and I have no yes/no question. So the moment the other
person says anything I haven't been taught — which is immediately, since my entire vocabulary is about 55
words — the exchange ends and I have no repair strategy whatsoever. The course gave me the ability to speak
and no ability to recover.

**The other structural holes, ranked by how much they'd hurt.** No past tense means I cannot narrate anything
that has already happened, which is most of what people talk about. No yes/no question means half of all
questions are closed to me. Only four verbs total (go, want, give, be), and "be" is mostly invisible. No "and",
so I cannot join two clauses. Numbers stop at five, which is useless for prices — I can ask *eshtu?* and then
not understand the answer, which is arguably worse than not asking. And no "toilet", which is the word every
traveller needs first.

**Where I'm shakier than I sound.** *Chennagilla* and *chikkadu* I have only ever read in a passing note —
never produced, never corrected on. I'd hesitate mid-sentence. The *hogu*/*hogi* politeness switch arrived in
lesson 10 as a remark and I've never used *hogi*; I'd probably say the blunt form to a stranger. *Kathe*
("story") I can recall but have used in exactly one two-word phrase and would never reach for it. And I'd
still have to think for a second before choosing *nanna* vs *nimma* — the app was right that they're near-twins
on the page, and the "your teacher means MY teacher" trap in lesson 5 rattled me more than it should have.

**Pronunciation: essentially unknown.** I have only ever typed. The respellings (`chehn-naa-gih-dee-nih`) are
the sole thing standing between me and being incomprehensible, and I have never once been corrected on how I
say anything. If a real Kannada speaker heard my *chennagiddeeni* I'd guess it lands somewhere between
recognisable and baffling, and nothing in ten lessons would have told me which.
