# Kannada playtest — lessons 5, 6, 9

Adult beginner, English speaker, no Indian languages. Went in with lessons 1-4 for the L5/L6 sessions and 1-8 for L9. Sessions `K3rA5`, `K3rA6`, `K3rA9`.

## 1. The "your" problem — this is the big one

This is the thing I'd fix first. Lesson 5 teaches me `nanna` = my and `nimma` = your, and then spends the rest of the lesson writing prompts in English where I genuinely cannot tell which one is wanted.

**First hit.** After teaching both words, it said:

> Say 'Your book'.

I stopped. Miko is the one talking to me. Does "your book" mean *my* book (Miko addressing me) or *Miko's* book (me addressing Miko)? I answered wrong on purpose to see what would happen, and asked on the same line:

> me: `Nanna pustaka - wait, when you say 'your book' do you mean the book belonging to me, or the one belonging to you?`
>
> Tutor: That is the note just above — have another look, and ask again if it is still unclear.
> Tutor: Not quite. Hint: Your + book. Say 'Your book'.

Two problems. The question got brushed off with a canned "look at the note above" — and the note above was about word order, not about whose "your" it is. And the hint, "Your + book", just repeats the ambiguous English back at me. That hint is worth nothing when the ambiguity *is* the word "your".

**Second hit, and this one is worse.** A few turns later:

> Point at a book across the room and tell Miko that the book is HERS.

Clear enough, I thought — hers, respectful, `avara`.

> me: `Adu avara pustaka`
>
> Tutor: Not quite. avara means "His / her (respectful)" — here you want the word for "Your".

The prompt says **HERS** in capitals and the correction says it wants **"Your"**. From where I sit that is a flat contradiction. I pushed back:

> me: `hang on, you asked me to say the book is HERS but the correction says you want 'Your'. Which one do you actually want?`
>
> Tutor: 💡 Watch which "your" an English prompt means. *Nimma* is the person you are TALKING TO — here, Miko. When a prompt says "your teacher" meaning the one who teaches YOU, the word is *nanna*, "my".

So the answer was `Adu nimma pustaka` — because the "her" in the prompt *is* Miko, the person I'm speaking to. That's a real rule and it's a fine note, but it only appeared because I argued. And it doesn't actually cover the case that tripped me: the prompt used a *third-person* pronoun ("HERS") for the person I'm addressing. Nothing warns about that.

**Third hit, lesson 6.**

> Introduce your friend to Miko.

Literal reading: the friend belongs to Miko. Actual answer: `nanna`, because it's *my* friend. I took the literal reading deliberately and flagged it:

> me: `Ivaru nimma snehita — guessing here, 'your friend' reads to me like the friend belongs to you, Miko. Is that right?`
>
> Tutor: 💡 *Yaaru* goes at the end, like every question word you have met...
> Tutor: Not quite. nimma means "Your" — here you want the word for "My".

The tip that fired was about `yaaru` and had nothing to do with what I asked. The correction is a bare word-swap that never says *why* "your friend" means mine. A learner who wasn't deliberately probing would take away "nimma is sometimes just wrong" and have no rule.

**Verdict:** yes, I was left guessing, repeatedly, and the prompt alone was never enough. Three prompts I'd quote as unfixable from the prompt text: `Say 'Your book'.` / `Point at a book across the room and tell Miko that the book is HERS.` / `Introduce your friend to Miko.` The one good note about it exists but is buried behind an argument, and the two prompts that most need it don't trigger it.

Related smaller thing, same lesson: `Say 'His story'.` doesn't say whether the "he" is senior to me, so `aatana` vs `avara` is a coin flip. I guessed `aatana` and it was accepted, but I didn't know it would be.

## 2. Lesson 6, ivaru vs avaru

This one is done well, and it's a noticeable contrast with the "your" mess.

The teaching moment for `ivaru`:

> **Ivaru** is "this person" — and also how you say "he" or "she" about someone standing in front of you. Kannada has no separate polite word for either, so **Ivaru** is what a prompt saying "he" or "she" wants. Same near/far vowel: *avaru* is "that person".

And two turns later, when `avaru` was formally introduced:

> **Avaru** is "that person" — someone further off. Same near/far vowel you have met twice already: *idu*/*adu*, *illi*/*alli*, and now *ivaru*/**Avaru**.

That is exactly the connection I wanted. I'd actually asked for it one turn early —

> me: `Snehita — is the i/a swap in ivaru vs avaru the same one as idu vs adu from lesson 2?`

— and got the canned `ivaru` note replayed at me verbatim instead of an answer. Then it turned up on its own the very next turn. So the content is there; the live-question handling just isn't. It came back a third time at the end:

> Third time that vowel has done the same job: *ivaru yaaru?* asks about the person beside you, *avaru yaaru?* about the one across the room. Once you have noticed the i-/a- pattern you can predict it rather than memorise it.

Three passes over the same pattern, tied to two things I already knew. That's the best-taught thing in these three lessons.

One ambiguous drill: `Someone points at a person. Ask who they are.` — near or far isn't stated. I answered `Ivaru yaaru?` and flagged the guess, and this time it actually answered me:

> That's a great question! Ivaru means 'this person' and is used for someone close to you. Avaru means 'that person' and is used for someone further away.

Nice, though it dodged the bit I asked (would `avaru` also have been accepted?).

## 4. Lesson 9, adjectives — can I say all three?

Short answer: **yes, all three, and the lesson is genuinely good on this** — but the "food is bad" drill contradicts its own teaching.

The split is taught up front and hard:

> "Good" is **Olleya** — but only ever in FRONT of a noun: *olleya oota*, "good food". It cannot end a sentence.

I asked immediately, because that's obviously a hole:

> me: `Olleya — so how do I say 'the food is good' then, if olleya can't end a sentence?`

and got the "bad" half first, then the good half one turn later:

> To say something IS good, you need **Chennagide** — and you already know both halves: the *chennagi* from *chennagiddeeni* ("I am well") and the *ide* from lesson 3 ("it is"). *Olleya* cannot do this job.

> "Bad" is **Ketta**, and like *olleya* it only sits in FRONT of a noun: *ketta oota*. To say something IS bad, Kannada mostly says it is not good — *chennagilla*, which is *chennagi* plus the *illa* ("not") from lesson 4.

So both halves of the pattern are there, and both are built out of things I already had. `olleya oota` / `oota chennagide` / `ketta oota` / `oota chennagilla`. It even threw in `chikkadu` at the very end ("my house IS small" = `nanna mane chikkadu`) so I can see the split isn't a one-off for "good". This is the clearest teaching in the three lessons.

**The bug.** The drill:

> The food is bad. Tell Miko you don't want it.

I wrote what the lesson had just taught me:

> me: `Oota chennagilla, oota beda`
>
> Tutor: Not quite. The word for "Bad (before a noun)" is missing.

So the answer key demands `ketta` — the *before-a-noun* word — for a prompt whose first sentence is "The food **is** bad". The lesson had explicitly told me `ketta` cannot do that job. I said so:

> me: `I'm confused — you told me ketta only sits in FRONT of a noun and that to say something IS bad I should use chennagilla. So how do I say 'the food is bad' as a full sentence?`
>
> Tutor: 💡 *Ketta* in front of the noun, *beda* from lesson 4 on the end. To say the food IS bad rather than refuse it, you would say *oota chennagilla* — "not good" — built from the *illa* you already have.
> Tutor: Not quite. 2 of the words are missing.

Fine answer buried under a grading error (more on that below). The wanted answer was `Ketta oota beda` — "I don't want bad food". Which is a *different sentence* from the one the prompt's own first line describes. The prompt reads as two instructions and only the second is graded. If it means "say you don't want the bad food", it should say that.

So: the lesson gives me the full pattern, but the one drill that touches "is bad" grades against the half the lesson told me not to use there.

## 5. Wrong answer with an English question attached

Did this three times. The verdict came through **every time** — never swallowed, never silently re-asked. The question is what got dropped.

1. `Nanna pustaka - wait, when you say 'your book' do you mean the book belonging to me, or the one belonging to you?` → "That is the note just above — have another look" **+** "Not quite. Hint: Your + book." Verdict clear. Question deflected to a note that didn't address it.
2. `Aatana hesaru — is aatana ok for a woman too, or is that only for men?` → "Not quite. aatana means "His" — here you want the word for "His / her (respectful)"." Verdict clear. Question ignored completely, not even a deflection.
3. `Ivaru nimma snehita — guessing here, 'your friend' reads to me like the friend belongs to you, Miko. Is that right?` → an unrelated `yaaru` tip, then "Not quite. nimma means "Your" — here you want the word for "My"." Verdict clear. Question ignored, tip irrelevant.

The failure mode is the mirror image of what I was told to look for. Verdicts survive; **questions get eaten**. And one worse case — a message that was *only* a question, no answer attempt at all, got graded as a wrong answer:

> me: `I'm confused — you told me ketta only sits in FRONT of a noun...`
>
> Tutor: Not quite. 2 of the words are missing.

Being told I'm wrong for asking a question is not great. "2 of the words are missing" is also opaque — which two?
