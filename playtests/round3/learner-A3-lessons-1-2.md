# Telugu, lessons 1 and 2 — learner A3, round 3

*Written as the learner. I had never seen a word of Telugu before this.*

## How it went, honestly

The good news first: I finished both lessons and I can actually say things. That's more than I expected. By the end of lesson 1 I could greet someone, say I was fine, and ask them how they were, unprompted, without looking anything up. By the end of lesson 2 I could point at things and ask what they were. Six words a lesson is a comfortable bite. The pace never felt like a slog and the little grammar notes are genuinely the best part — the one explaining that *Bagunnanu* is *baga* + *unnanu* glued together, with the vowel dropping out, made the whole word stop being a random blob of syllables. Same with the note that the verb ending is what carries the person (*unnanu* / *unnaru* / *unnavu*). That's the kind of thing I'd have asked a human teacher and been pleased to get.

What I could use unprompted at the end:

- **Namaskaram, nenu bagunnanu, meeru ela unnaru?** — I put that whole three-part exchange together myself when asked, and got it right first try.
- **Idhi emiti? / Adhi emiti?** — pointing at a near thing or a far thing and asking what it is.
- **Idhi pusthakam.** — this is a book.
- **Meeru peru emiti?** — asking a name. Though see below, I'm not sure this is actually right.

The bad news: **I asked five questions across the two lessons and got a straight answer to exactly zero of them at the time.** Three were bundled onto correct answers and were silently dropped, as if I hadn't typed them. One got "I will come back to that" and it never came back. And the one time the tutor did answer a question — after the lesson was over — it told me something that flatly contradicted what it had taught me twenty minutes earlier. More on that.

## Where I got stuck

**1. "Ask Miko how she is doing."** I typed `Meeru ela unnaru?` — the exact sentence the tutor had marked **"Correct!"** two steps before for "How are you?". This time: **"Not quite. Hint: Name + you + how?"**. So the difference was that it wanted Miko's name in front. Fine, but nothing told me that, and the hint itself is confusing — "Name + you + how?" has no verb in it, so if I'd followed the hint literally I'd have written *Miko meeru ela?* and been wrong again. I guessed `Miko, meeru ela unnaru?` and that passed. This is the only place I actually failed something, and it felt like a gotcha rather than a lesson.

**2. "Combine 'You' (from last lesson) and 'Name What?'."** I had to stop and translate the instruction before I could translate the Telugu. It's an English word-puzzle, not a task. I wrote back "I think you mean 'what is your name'" and guessed `Meeru peru emiti?`, which passed — and the note then told me that in real Telugu there's a possessive *Mee* I haven't met and that stringing *You + Name + What* together is only the casual version. So the drill taught me a sentence it immediately told me isn't quite the real thing. I'd rather have been taught *Mee*.

**3. "Say 'I am fine, and you?'"** — I had no word for "and". I said so in plain English: *"I don't know how to say 'and' — you haven't taught me that."* The tutor accepted my half-answer, moved on, and the grammar note handed me the model sentence (*Nenu bagunnanu, meeru?*) — which turns out not to need "and" at all. So the honest "I don't know" was handled kindly. But it also passed me on a sentence I hadn't produced, which felt a bit like being waved through.

**4. I typed just "ok"** at one prompt, to see what happened. **"Not quite. Hint: Direct answer."** Clear and fair, no complaint there.

## Things asked that were never taught

- **"and"** — required by a sentence, never given (see above).
- **Miko's name as part of the answer** — the task was "ask Miko how she is doing" and the grader wanted the name inside the Telugu, which no example had shown.
- **Ee** was taught and then never used. It's in the "you can now say" summary, but I was only ever asked to repeat the bare word once. I never built a sentence with it in the whole lesson. Afterwards I asked about it directly, and the tutor said *"Now, try to use Ee in a sentence"* — so even it seemed to know the drill was missing.
- **aa** ("that", before a noun) was mentioned in passing in a grammar note and then never appeared again, and isn't in the end-of-lesson summary at all, even though *Ee* is. Loose end.

## The thing that actually confused me

In lesson 2 the tutor taught me this, clearly:

> "Before a noun, 'this' changes shape: **Ee**. *Idhi* stands alone — *Idhi pusthakam*, 'this is a book' — but *Ee pusthakam* is 'this book'."

Good. That's a grammar rule: *Idhi* is the standalone pronoun, *Ee* is the version that sits in front of a noun. And separately, *Adhi* is "that — something further away". Distance is the *idhi/adhi* axis; standalone-vs-attached is the *idhi/ee* axis. I understood it.

Then, after the lesson, I asked the question I'd been sitting on the whole time — *"Why is it 'ee' sometimes and 'idhi' other times?"* — and got:

> "Idhi is used for something that is a bit farther away, while Ee is for something very close. For example, if you see a book right next to you, you say 'Ee pusthakam.' But if the book is a little further away, you would say 'Idhi pusthakam.'"

That is not what the lesson said. It's turned a grammatical distinction into a distance distinction, and it contradicts the lesson's own example (which glossed *Idhi pusthakam* as "this is a book", not "that book over there") and the summary card I'd just been shown ("Ee — This (before a noun)"). If I'd only had that answer I'd now believe something wrong about Telugu. As a learner that's the single worst thing in this playtest — I no longer know which of the two explanations to trust.

## How it felt / would I continue

I'd continue, yes — the drilling works and I retained the words. But I'd continue with a notebook and a nagging feeling that I can't ask this teacher anything. The lessons are a well-built conveyor belt and the moment you step off it to ask "why", either nothing happens or you get an answer that may be made up. A human tutor who ignored me five times running would have lost me.

## The eight things I was asked to check

### 1. Correct answer marked wrong when an English question is attached

**Not reproduced. Looks fixed.** I tried it four times, at different stages, and every single time the correct Telugu was accepted:

- Vocab stage — `Unnaru — why does 'you are' need a separate word if Meeru already means you?` → **"Good. It is spelled **Unnaru**."** and moved on.
- Review stage — `Unnaru. Also, is Bagunnanu related to Unnaru? They both end in -nnanu / -nnaru.` → **"🎓 Review passed"**.
- Vocab stage L2 — `Ee — why is it ee sometimes and idhi other times?` → **"Yes. It is spelled **Ee**."**
- Roleplay stage L2 — `Adhi emiti? — and while we're here, does 'aa' work the same way as 'ee' before a noun?` → **"Great job!"**

No "correct and Not quite in the same turn" anywhere. The credit half of that bug is genuinely fixed.

**But the other half isn't.** In all four cases the question was **silently discarded** — no answer, no "I'll come back to it", no acknowledgement that I'd typed anything beyond the drill word. And when I asked a question on its own line with no answer attached, mid-lesson, I got a promise that was never kept: **"I will come back to that."** followed by **"So — what's the Telugu word for 'What'?"** and then nothing, ever. So from where I sit, asking a question is still broken; it's just broken quietly now instead of loudly.

### 2. Tutor stating an answer the grader then rejects

**Not reproduced.** Nothing like the "chesa" / "chesanu" case. Every word the tutor stated was accepted when I typed it back, including the clipped alternates it volunteered (it said *bagunna* was "accepted here" — I didn't test that one, but nothing contradicted it).

The nearest thing was #1 under "Where I got stuck": the tutor said **"Correct!"** to `Meeru ela unnaru?` as "How are you?", then two steps later marked the identical string **"Not quite."** for "Ask Miko how she is doing." Not the same bug — the task changed — but it lands on the learner the same way: *you told me this was right.*

### 3. Tutor replying in Telugu / using untaught Telugu

**Reproduced, badly.** Every question I asked in session 1 after the lesson finished came back as a wall of Telugu I have no way to read:

> **"Nenu dani gurinchi cheppagalanu, kani nenu cheptanu. "Ela unnaru" anedi correct phrase. Please ask your next question."**

> **"Nenu dani gurinchi cheppagalaru, kani naku teliyadu. Nenu malli cheptanu. Please ask your next question."**

> **"Nenu dani gurinchi cheppagalanu, kani nenu cheptanu. "Meeru ela unnaru" anedi correct phrase. Please ask your next question."**

Three for three. Of the words in there I had been taught exactly two — *nenu* and *unnaru*. **dani, gurinchi, cheppagalanu, cheppagalaru, kani, naku, teliyadu, malli, cheptanu, anedi** — never seen any of them. And it doesn't even hold together as a sentence: the same template comes out as *"cheppagalanu, kani nenu cheptanu"* one time and *"cheppagalaru, kani naku teliyadu"* the next, which reads like a template being filled at random. It also says "Please ask your next question" and is then immediately followed by **"That's the scenario done — nothing left to answer here."** in the same turn.

Worth noting this did **not** happen in session 2 — there the post-lesson answers came back in fluent English. Same command, same point in the flow, different behaviour. So it's intermittent, which makes it worse, not better.

### 4. Two different tasks in one turn

**Partly.** Twice per lesson the tutor teaches two new words in one message and only drills one:

> "🐾 "I'm fine" is **Bagunnanu**. "How" is **Ela**. Your turn — say **Ela**"

> "🐾 "Book" is **Pusthakam**. "Name" is **Peru**. Your turn — say **Peru**"

Which one is scored is clear ("Your turn — say Ela"), so I never got confused about what to type. But the pronunciation hints stack up oddly at the end — `[say it like: Bagunnanu: baa-gun-naa-nu]  [say it like: Ela: eh-laa]` — two bracketed blocks in a row, when the single-word turns get one clean one. Reads like a seam in the machine.

The one genuine contradiction-in-one-turn is post-lesson: **"Now, try to use Ee in a sentence with something you see around you."** immediately followed by **"That's the scenario done — nothing left to answer here."** It asked me to do something and told me there was nothing to do, in the same breath. (I tried anyway — `Ee pusthakam` — and it just echoed it back with a translation.)

### 5. Tutor handing me the answer

**Yes, repeatedly — and once it flat-out spoiled a later drill.**

The clearest case: when teaching *Ee*, three steps into lesson 2, the tutor said **"*Idhi* stands alone — *Idhi pusthakam*, "this is a book""**. That is verbatim the answer to a drill I was given later — **"Say 'This is a book'."** I didn't have to know anything; I scrolled up. *Pusthakam* itself hadn't even been formally taught yet at that point.

The other cases are the grammar notes, which routinely contain the model answer to the step I just did — e.g. after my incomplete attempt at "I am fine, and you?" the note supplied **"*Nenu bagunnanu, meeru?* is the natural form here"**, and the note after "ask what is your name" supplied the structure. That's less harmful because it lands *after* my answer, but combined with #3 above ("I don't know 'and'" → passed anyway) it means you can get through a step without producing the sentence and still be shown the sentence.

### 6. Typos — forgiven sensibly? Shown the right spelling?

**Forgiven sensibly, yes. Shown the spelling — only in the vocab stage.** Three typos, all from the pronunciation hint the way a real person would fumble them:

- `Meru` (from *mee-ru*) → **"Yes. It is spelled **Meeru**."** — perfect. Credited, corrected, moved on.
- `Emithi` (from *ay-mih-ti*) → **"Good. It is spelled **Emiti**."** — same, good.
- `Idhi pustakam` (dropped the h) in the roleplay stage → **"Perfect!"** and nothing else. No correction, no spelling shown.

So the correction only fires during vocab drilling. Once you're in sentences, a misspelling sails through unremarked, which is exactly where I'd most want to be told, because that's the spelling I'll walk away with.

One oddity: the "It is spelled X" line also fires when I spelled it **correctly** — `Unnaru — why does...` got **"Good. It is spelled **Unnaru**."** and `Ee — why is it...` got **"Yes. It is spelled **Ee**."** I hadn't got either wrong. I suspect the attached English question is being read as a misspelling. Harmless, mildly patronising.

### 7. Pronunciation hints vs the spelling you must type

**They agree, and the gaps are the normal romanisation ones.** No case where the hint pointed at a different word than the one I had to type. Where I tripped, the hint was fair-but-lossy rather than wrong:

- `Meeru [mee-ru]` — nothing in "mee-ru" says the *ee* is doubled in writing. I typed *Meru*.
- `Emiti [ay-mih-ti]` — "ti" vs "thi" is invisible in that hint; Telugu romanisation uses both and I picked wrong. Same story with `Pusthakam [pus-tuh-kum]`, where the silent *h* in *sth* has no representative in the hint at all — and note that *pus-tuh-kum* also loses the final *-am*.
- `Namaskaram [nuh-mus-kaa-rum]`, `Nenu [nay-nu]`, `Peru [pay-ru]`, `Idhi [ih-dhih]`, `Adhi [uh-dhih]`, `Ela [eh-laa]`, `Ee [ee]` — all fine.

Also — every hint is romanised English syllables and I was never shown the Telugu script once, in either lesson. I'm not complaining as a beginner, but I should probably know that's the deal.

### 8. Stray internal text

**None.** No `null`, no `undefined`, no `[not simulated]`, no placeholder tokens, no leaked brackets or template braces anywhere in either session.

The closest thing to a seam is the doubled pronunciation block on two-word turns (`[say it like: Bagunnanu: ...]  [say it like: Ela: ...]`, note the double space) and the malformed Telugu template in #3 — but neither is literal debug text.
