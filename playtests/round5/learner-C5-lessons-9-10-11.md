# Lessons 9, 10, 11 — how it actually went

Learner C5. English speaker, no Telugu outside this course. Came in off lessons 1–8.
Played 9 (adjectives), 10 ("review & survival dialogue"), 11 (present continuous), fresh session each.
Sessions: `r5c9`, `r5c10`, `r5c11`. All three completed in exactly 15 steps, no loops, no dead ends.

---

## The short version

Lesson 9 was the best of the three. Lesson 11 was the most interesting and the most frustrating.
Lesson 10 is not a review of anything — it's five new restaurant words with a review label on it.

The mechanics work. Nothing crashed, nothing showed me `null` or `{w}`, and I never got stuck in a
repeat loop. The problems are all about *teaching*: a couple of turns hand me two words at once and
only drill one, my questions were almost entirely ignored (and one of them was scored as a wrong
answer), one drill asked me for a word I'd never been given at all, and lesson 11 gives me a pattern
I can admire but can't actually use on a new verb.

---

## Lesson 9 — Basic Adjectives

This one landed. Pedda, chinna, manchi, chedu, vedi, bagundi. Six adjectives, and by the end I was
genuinely building things: *pedda illu*, *manchi snehithudu*, *vedi neeru*, *idhi pedda pusthakam*,
*ayana manchi guruvu*, *adhi chedu*. All of those reused nouns from my old notebook — illu, snehithudu,
neeru, pusthakam, guruvu, ayana, idhi, adhi. That felt great. It's the first lesson where the new
material multiplied against the old material instead of sitting next to it.

The grammar note about there being no word for "is" was the single most useful thing in three lessons.
*Adhi chedu* = "that bad" = "that is bad". Once I had that, every sentence in the back half was
obvious. It arrived at the first review question, well before I needed it to build sentences. Good
timing.

**But it fired five separate times, word for word, all four hundred characters of it.** By the fifth
time I was skimming past it. Same for the *bagundi* note, which got shown once inline and then
repeated verbatim on the very next turn. If it's already been said and I got the answer right, don't
say it again.

**Two words in one turn.** Step 5 read:

> "Hot" is **Vedi**. **Bagundi** is "it is good" — ... Your turn — say **Bagundi**
> [say it like: Vedi — vay-dih, Bagundi — baa-gun-dih]

So I got taught two words, told to say one, and handed a doubled pronunciation hint with both crammed
in. I asked "do I need to say that one too?" and got no answer. I never once had to produce *vedi*
during the teaching phase — the first time I typed it was in the review quiz two turns later, and I
only got it right because I'd noticed it in passing. If I'd skimmed that turn I'd have been asked for
a word I'd never practised.

Also, that note ends "This is the word for a meal, a day, a film." Reading it cold, I couldn't tell
what "this" pointed at — the sentence before it is about *baga* and *undi*, so on first read I thought
it meant *undi*. It means *bagundi*. Took me a second pass.

One small contradiction I noticed and it did nag: the *bagundi* note says it's *baga* + *undi*
("it is"), and then the very next note says *undi* is **not** for joining a noun to a describing word.
So which is it? I decided it was a frozen expression and moved on, but a beginner asking "is undi the
'is' word or not" gets two different answers in consecutive turns.

**Where I lost credit unfairly:** I asked a question *instead* of answering — "before I answer, how
would I say 'very good'? Is it chala manchi?" — and got back "**Not quite.** Hint: He + good +
teacher." My question was treated as a wrong attempt at the drill. That's the worst single moment in
the three lessons. It teaches me that curiosity is punished and I should keep my mouth shut. (I still
don't know if *chala manchi* is right.)

## Lesson 10 — "Review & Survival Dialogue"

Billed as a review. It is not a review. Turn one is a brand new word (*bhojanam*), and so are turns
two through six. Five new words: bhojanam, billu, ivvandi, dhanyavaadaalu, kurchondi. There is no
step anywhere in the lesson that goes back and checks something from lessons 1–9.

Old vocabulary does *appear* — I used *kaavali* and *neeru* to order, *bagundi* and *ee* for "this
meal is good", *namaskaram* at the end — but I was never quizzed on any of it, never had a word from
an earlier lesson pulled up and tested. It reviews by accident, through the sentences, not on purpose.
If someone came into this lesson shaky on lessons 5–8, they'd leave exactly as shaky.

I'd also flag that four of the five new words are near-freebies for an English speaker. *Billu* is
"bill". *Kurchondi* I guessed instantly off *kurchilu* = chairs from lesson 8. *Dhanyavaadaalu* is
the only one that took real effort, and it's five syllables. So the actual learning load here is
low — which would be fine for a review lesson, except it isn't one.

**The thing that was never taught: "and".** Step 11 said "Order a meal and water." I have no word for
"and". It has never appeared in nine lessons. I said so in plain English:

> You: I don't know how to say 'and' in Telugu — you've never taught me that word. Can you tell me?
> Tutor: "Bhojanam kaavali, neeru kaavali".
> Tutor: **Not quite.** Hint: Meal + want + water + want.

Two things wrong in one turn. First, **the tutor stated the answer and then marked me wrong in the
same breath** — two tutor lines back to back, one giving me the sentence, the next scoring my turn as
a failure. Confusing to read: am I right or wrong? Second, the actual answer is that Telugu here just
repeats the verb and uses a comma — which is a genuinely interesting fact and *should have been a
grammar note before the drill*, not a hint after I'd been marked down. I only got it right on the
retry by copy-pasting what the tutor had just leaked.

**Where the grammar note was late:** I asked at step 3 whether the *-andi* on *ivvandi* was the same
politeness thing as *vellandi* from lesson 3. No answer. The note explaining exactly that —
"That -andi ending is what makes any Telugu command polite. You will reuse it on every verb." —
showed up at step 9, six turns later, after I'd already guessed my way through *billu ivvandi*. It's a
good note. It answered my question. It just arrived long after I'd asked, with no acknowledgement that
it was an answer.

**Dropped pronoun:** "I want a meal" I answered as *bhojanam kaavali*, no *naaku*. Accepted, marked
"Phrases done". Good — that's the natural way to say it and I wasn't punished for leaving out an
optional word.

## Lesson 11 — Present Continuous. The honest answer on productivity.

**No. I cannot put a new verb into this form. I can only repeat the three I was handed.**

Let me be precise about why, because the lesson genuinely tries.

The note at step 5 is good: *Unnanu* is "I am", it's the tail of every word in the lesson, *chestu* +
*unnanu* = "I am doing", and swapping the tail swaps the person — *unnanu* = I, *unnaru* = you. That's
a real rule, stated clearly, and it arrived before I had to build anything. The follow-up note
("the -nu on the end is the I") reinforced it.

So the **person** ending I can do. Off that note I'd confidently produce *tintunnaru* for "you are
eating," and I said so mid-lesson.

What I cannot do is the other half — **getting from a verb to its stem.** Look at what I have:

- ches-tunnanu
- tin-tunnanu
- vel-**th**unnanu

Three stems, and one of them takes *-thunnanu* while the other two take *-tunnanu*. Nobody explained
that. I have exactly one base verb in my whole notebook, *vellu* (go, lesson 3), and its stem is
*vel*, so drop the *-lu*? Is that a rule? I have no second example to check it against. I don't know
the base form of "do" or "eat" at all — I was handed *chestunnanu* and *tintunnanu* as whole words
with no dictionary form behind them.

So if you ask me "I am reading" or "I am writing" or "I am sitting", I'm stuck twice over: I don't know
the verb, and even if you gave it to me I'd be flipping a coin between *-tunnanu* and *-thunnanu*. I
asked exactly this at the end:

> You: I still don't know how to make a NEW verb into this form. I only have the three you gave me.
> How would I say 'I am reading'?
> Tutor: That's the scenario done — nothing left to answer here.

That's the honest state I finished in. The lesson taught me three fixed phrases plus a genuine
insight about the *person* ending, and left the *stem* half of the pattern completely dark. One extra
note — "here is the base verb, here is how the stem comes off it" — with two worked examples would
have turned this from three memorised words into an actual generative tool.

What I *could* do unprompted by the end: *pani chestunnanu*, *ippudu velthunnanu*, *annam tintunnanu*,
*nenu pani chestunnanu*, *ippudu idhi chestunnanu*, *ippudu intiki velthunnanu*. All real sentences,
all recombinations of what I was given. Fine. Just not new verbs.

**One I want credit for and got:** "I am going home now" — I reached for *intiki* rather than *illu*,
because *intiki* is sitting in my notebook as an alternate of *illu* and looks like a "to the house"
form. It was accepted. But nobody ever *taught* me that *intiki* means "to the house" — it's listed as
a spelling variant of *illu*, which is misleading. I guessed right by pattern-sniffing, not because I
was taught. A learner who used *illu* there would presumably also have been accepted, and neither of
us would understand the difference.

**Two words in one turn, again.** Step 5, identical shape to lesson 9's step 5: teaches *Pani* and
*Unnanu*, drills only *Unnanu*, and the hint is the doubled
`[say it like: Pani — puh-nih, Unnanu — un-naa-nu]`. This looks like a structural thing in step 5 of
every lesson, not a one-off. *Pani* then showed up as the first review question — a word I'd been
shown but never asked to type.

**A repeated drill.** "Say 'I am doing work'" appears twice — once as a phrase step, then again five
steps later dressed up as "Miko asks what you're doing. Say 'I am doing work'." Same task, same
answer. In a 15-step lesson that's a wasted slot.

# The ten questions, answered

**1. Was every word a drill asked for actually taught to you, including changed forms?**

Words: almost. Two words — *Vedi* (L9) and *Pani* (L11) — were *shown* but never *drilled* during
teaching, because both arrived as the second word in a two-word step 5 where only the other word was
practised. Both then appeared as the first review question. Shown, so technically taught; never
rehearsed, so easy to miss.

One outright miss: **"and" was never taught and a drill required it** ("Order a meal and water", L10).
The construction — repeat the verb, comma between — is not in any lesson and was not explained before
the drill. I was marked wrong for saying I didn't know it.

Changed forms: **no.** L11 requires the present-continuous stem (*ches-*, *tin-*, *vel-*), and how you
get from a verb to that stem is never taught. *Intiki* ("to the house") is required in L11 and only
exists in my notebook as an alternate spelling of *illu*, never as a distinct form with a meaning.

**2. Did any rule you were graded on arrive only AFTER you'd been marked wrong?**

Yes, once, and it's the clearest failure in the set. The "repeat the verb instead of using 'and'" rule
was delivered *as a hint attached to my failing grade* in L10 step 11. I was marked "Not quite" for a
construction that had never been presented.

Softer case: the *-andi* politeness note arrived at L10 step 9, six turns after I asked and after I'd
already had to guess *billu ivvandi*. I wasn't penalised, but I was guessing.

**3. Was a correct answer ever marked wrong?**

Not a correct Telugu answer, no. Every correct answer scored correct, including:
- one with an English question attached (*"Vedi — is that hot like temperature or hot like spicy
  food?"* → "Spot on!")
- one with the optional pronoun dropped (*bhojanam kaavali* for "I want a meal") → accepted
- one with an optional pronoun *added* (*nenu pani chestunnanu*) → accepted

**But a pure question with no answer attached was graded as a wrong attempt** (L9: "how would I say
'very good'?" → "Not quite"). And in L10 I was marked "Not quite" for correctly reporting that I'd
never been taught the word the drill required. Neither is a mis-scored Telugu answer, but both are the
grader treating honest non-answers as failures.

**4. Were your questions answered?**

Overwhelmingly no. Ten asked, one answered (late, incidentally, unacknowledged), one partially
inferable from a replayed note, one refused, seven flatly ignored. One cost me a mark. Full table
above.

**5. Did the tutor reply in Telugu, or use Telugu you were never taught?**

Never replied *in* Telugu — all instruction was English, which is right for a beginner. Telugu words
appeared only as targets, hints, or notes.

Two edge cases: the L9 *bagundi* note cites *baga* ("well") as a component, which is a word I don't
have and can't use — fine as etymology, and it's labelled. The L11 note cites *unnaru* ("you are"),
which I *do* have from lesson 1. No unexplained Telugu was thrown at me.

**6. Did the tutor ever state an answer the grader then rejected, or contradict the lesson?**

**Yes to the first, in the same turn.** L10 step 11:

> Tutor: "Bhojanam kaavali, neeru kaavali".
> Tutor: Not quite. Hint: Meal + want + water + want.

The correct sentence is stated, then my turn is marked wrong, as two consecutive tutor lines. Reading
it, I couldn't tell whether I'd passed.

**A contradiction too,** across consecutive turns in L9. Note A: *bagundi* is *baga* + *undi*, where
*undi* = "it is". Note B: there is no word for "is"; *undi* is for existence/location, **not** for
joining a noun to a describing word. Both are defensible to a linguist; to a beginner they say
opposite things about what *undi* does, one turn apart.

**7. Typos — consistent, correct spelling shown, no false correction?**

Grading: consistent and generous — 4/4 typos accepted.
Correct spelling shown: **3 of 4.** `Dhanyavadalu` got no correction, on the longest and hardest word
in that lesson — the one where I most needed it. That's a real gap, and it's now sitting in my
notebook as an accepted variant, so I've learned the wrong spelling as legitimate.
False corrections: **none.** No word I spelled right was ever "fixed".

**8. Stray internal text?**

No `null`, no `undefined`, no `{w}`, no `[not simulated]`, no truncated lines. Two cosmetic issues:

- **Doubled/merged pronunciation hints**, twice, both at step 5:
  `[say it like: Vedi — vay-dih, Bagundi — baa-gun-dih]` (L9) and
  `[say it like: Pani — puh-nih, Unnanu — un-naa-nu]` (L11). Two words' hints crammed into one
  bracket for a turn that drills one word.
- **The same grammar note replayed verbatim up to five times** in L9 (the "no word for is" note) and
  three times in L11. Not stray text exactly, but it reads like a template firing on every step rather
  than a decision to remind me.
- A dangling referent: "This is the word for a meal, a day, a film" (L9 step 5) — "this" points three
  clauses back, past two other words.

**9. Two different tasks in one turn, or praise on a turn you never took?**

**Two tasks in one turn: yes, twice** — step 5 of both L9 and L11 teach two words and drill one.
Structurally the same both times, so likely by design rather than a glitch.

**Two tutor lines in one turn: yes, once** — L10 step 11, answer-then-rejection.

**Praise on a turn I never took: no.** Every "Correct/Perfect" followed a real attempt of mine.
Closest thing: L9 step 3, where I asked "do I need to say Vedi too?" alongside a correct *Bagundi* and
got approval — the approval was earned by the *Bagundi*, but it also reads as approval of a question
that was in fact ignored.

**10. Lesson 10 is billed as a review and survival dialogue — did it review anything?**

**No.** Nothing from lessons 1–9 is quizzed, revisited, or checked anywhere in the 15 steps. It is
five new restaurant words (bhojanam, billu, ivvandi, dhanyavaadaalu, kurchondi) run through the
identical teach → memory-check → phrases → conversation shape as every other lesson.

Earlier vocabulary does get *used* in the sentence-building half — *kaavali*, *neeru*, *ee*,
*bagundi*, *namaskaram* — so old words are exercised in passing. But there's no retrieval practice on
them: I'm never asked "what was the word for X" about anything I learned before this lesson. Someone
shaky on lessons 5–8 finishes this lesson exactly as shaky.

The "survival dialogue" half is more honest — ordering, asking for the bill, complimenting the meal,
thanking — and that sequence genuinely felt usable. Rename the lesson, or add three or four retrieval
questions from earlier lessons to the memory-check phase.
