# Learner B5 — Telugu lessons 3, 4, 5

Round 5 playtest. I'd finished lessons 1 and 2, so going in I knew: namaskaram, nenu,
meeru/nuvvu, unnaru/unnavu, bagunnanu, ela, idhi, adhi, ee, emiti, pusthakam, peru.
Nothing else. I played 3, 4 and 5 back to back as fresh sessions (`r5b3`, `r5b4`, `r5b5`).
All three finished, 15/15 steps each.

## How it went, honestly

Lesson 3 was the best-taught of the three and also the one that annoyed me most. Lesson 4
was smooth and slightly empty. Lesson 5 was where I got graded unfairly twice and got
genuinely stuck for three turns on something I'd answered correctly.

By the end I can say six or seven real sentences without thinking: *illu ekkada undi?*,
*akkada vellu*, *akkada vellandi*, *illu akkada undi*, *neeru kaavali*, *annam oddu*,
*naa peru...*, *mee pusthakam*, *ame peru*, *adhi mee pusthakam*, *idhi naa katha*. That's
a real gain and it felt good. The A/B pattern "thing first, then the little word that does
the work" clicked properly and I could feel myself producing new combinations rather than
recalling stock phrases — *ayana katha* and *idhi naa katha* I built myself and got right
first try.

But three specific things that were *presented as the point of the lesson* I cannot
actually use, because I was never asked to use them once. More on that in Q10.

### Where I got stuck

1. **"Say 'I am here'" (lesson 3).** Ten seconds earlier the tutor had told me, emphatically,
   that *undi* is "for things, not people". So I had no verb at all for a person. I said so in
   plain English — "I don't think you've taught me 'am' for a person... is it just *nenu
   ikkada*?" — and got back **"Correct!"** and moved on. That's praise for a paragraph of
   English with a guess buried in it. I still don't know if *nenu ikkada* is right; I only
   later inferred it probably is, because the tutor itself wrote *Meeru ekkada?* with no
   verb. Nothing ever confirmed it.

2. **"Go there" (lesson 3).** I tried *vellu akkada* (English order). Marked wrong. The
   word-order rule that makes it wrong — verb last, Telugu is SOV — appeared for the first
   time in the hint on that failure, and then in full as the closing note of the entire
   lesson, five steps later. So the rule I was graded on arrived because I broke it.

3. **"Introduce yourself: 'Hello, my name is...'" (lesson 5).** This is the bad one. I
   said `Namaskaram, naa peru Sam`. Marked **wrong**. I asked what was wrong with it —
   ignored, marked wrong again. On the third try I dropped my name entirely and said
   `Namaskaram, naa peru`, which is an incomplete sentence, and got **"Exactly!"** —
   followed by "Note the spelling: **Namaskaram, naa peru Ravi**." So it wanted the name
   **Ravi**, a name nobody ever gave me, in a drill that explicitly asked me to introduce
   *myself*. Three turns burned and the "correction" taught me a word that isn't a word.

4. **"Identify a book as 'your book'" (lesson 5).** I said *mee pusthakam*. Wrong. The hint
   then revealed it wanted "That + your + book", i.e. *adhi mee pusthakam*. The prompt said
   "your book"; the answer was "that is your book". That's the prompt being wrong, not me.

### Things used on me before I was taught them

- *ikkada* and *illu* both appear in the example sentence *"Illu ikkada undi"* — the
  lesson-3 note for **undi** — while teaching *undi*, which is step 3. *ikkada* is taught at
  step 4 and *illu* at step 5. I asked outright "what does ikkada mean? you used it in that
  example but I've never seen it" and got the same note re-pasted at me, verbatim, with the
  unexplained word still in it.
- *pani* ("work") → *paniki* is dropped into the -ki grammar note as the illustration and
  never appears again. Fine as an example, but it's a Telugu word I now half-know and
  can't check.
- **Akkada** (lesson 3) and **Ame** (lesson 5) are each announced in a turn that drills a
  *different* word, so neither ever gets a "your turn — say it" step. Both then show up in
  the recall quiz. I happened to remember them; I'd have been irritated if I hadn't.

### Two-words-in-one-turn bug, and the doubled hint

This happened once in lesson 3 and once in lesson 5, identically:

> "There" is **Akkada**. "House" is **Illu**. Going TO it, the word reshapes and takes -ki...
> Your turn — say **Illu**  [say it like: Akkada — uhk-kuh-duh, Illu — ihl-lu]

> "Her" is **Ame**. "Story" is **Katha**. Your turn — say **Katha**
> [say it like: Ame — aa-meh, Katha — kuh-thuh]

Two new words, one grammar rule, and one drill, all in a single turn — and the pronunciation
hint is two hints merged into one bracket, including a hint for the word I'm *not* being
asked to say. Every other turn's hint is a bare `[say it like: x]`, so this reads as a bug.

### Grammar notes — did they land, and were they on time?

- **-andi politeness (L3, on time).** Landed. I used *vellandi* unprompted later and it was
  accepted. Good note.
- **undi = things not people (L3, on time but incomplete).** Landed as a restriction, but
  it creates a hole the lesson then walks me straight into (see stuck #1). Telling me what
  *doesn't* work without telling me what does is worse than saying nothing.
- **illu → inti + -ki (L3, on time, never practised).** I understood it as a fact. I asked
  twice how far it generalises ("is that a rule I can apply elsewhere?", "does katha change
  its ending like illu became inti?") and got no answer either time. "Telugu does that to
  most nouns" is not enough to act on — *most* is the problem word.
- **Question words go to the end (L3, arrived after the drill).** I'd already answered
  *illu ekkada undi?* correctly by copying the model sentence when the note appeared. Lucky.
  Also I'd say the note overclaims: "**every** Telugu question word does" is a very strong
  statement to hand a beginner with no exceptions flagged.
- **SOV / undi does real work (L3, arrived last, after I'd been marked wrong on it).** This
  is the single most useful note in the three lessons and it is the very last line of the
  lesson. Move it to before "Go there".
- **Naaku / Telugu has no "have" (L5, on time).** Best note of the set — *naaku pusthakam
  undi* immediately made sense and it retroactively explained the *undi* restriction from
  lesson 3. Then it's never drilled. See Q10.
- **Ayana is the respectful form (L5, late and misplaced).** It appeared after the recall
  quiz had already asked me for "He (respectful) / his" — a label using a word the teaching
  turn never said — and it landed on the turn where I'd answered *"Naa peru"*, so I got a
  note about *ayana* as feedback for a sentence with no *ayana* in it.
- **Lesson 4 had no grammar notes at all.** Zero 💡 lines. The only explanation of how
  *kaavali* works came because I asked. A learner who doesn't ask gets five words and no
  structure, and is then expected to produce *sare, idhi kaavali*.

### How it felt

Lessons 3 and 4 felt friendly and quick. Lesson 5 felt like arguing with a machine. Being
marked wrong for putting my own name in "introduce yourself", asking why, being ignored, and
then being told the right answer contains a stranger's name — that's the moment I'd have
closed the app. The bigger quiet frustration is that questions mostly vanish. Six of my
eight questions got either silence or a canned note I'd already read, so I stopped expecting
answers and just fed the machine what it wanted. That's the opposite of what a tutor is for.

## The ten questions

**1. Was every word a drill asked for actually taught to me, including changed forms?**

Mostly yes, with real exceptions.

- Every word the *recall quizzes* and *sentence drills* asked for had been shown at least
  once — but **Akkada** and **Ame** were only ever *shown*, never drilled, because they were
  bundled into another word's turn. They were then quizzed.
- **Not taught and required: the name "Ravi"** in lesson 5's introduce-yourself drill. That
  is a word the grader demanded and nobody ever taught. Worst offender in the set.
- **Not taught and required: a copula for a person.** Lesson 3 asks for "I am here" having
  explicitly ruled out the only "is" I own.
- Changed forms: *vellandi* was taught and accepted. *inti / intiki* was taught in a note
  and never asked for. *naaku* was taught and never asked for. So no changed form was ever
  demanded before being shown — the failure is the reverse, shown and never demanded.

**2. Did any rule I was graded on arrive only AFTER I'd been marked wrong?**

Yes, once, clearly: **verb-final word order**. I was marked wrong on *vellu akkada*, and the
"There + go" hint on that failure was the first time order was mentioned. The full SOV note
came at the very end of the lesson.

Two near-misses where I got lucky rather than taught: question-word-final position (note
arrived after the drill I'd guessed right), and "*adhi* + possessive + noun" for lesson 5's
"identify a book as your book" — I only learned that shape from the failure hint.

**3. Was a correct answer ever marked wrong?**

Yes, twice, both in lesson 5.

- `Namaskaram, naa peru Sam` — correct Telugu, exactly what "introduce yourself" asks for,
  rejected because the grader wanted the literal string with **Ravi**. Cost me three turns.
- `Mee pusthakam` for the prompt "Identify a book as 'your book'" — rejected for missing an
  *adhi* the prompt never asked for.

On the two specific cases you asked about:

- **English question attached to a correct answer: never penalised.** `Vellu — is vellandi
  the form...`, `Illu. Why does it become inti...`, `Akkada vellu — so does the verb always
  come last...`, `Sare, neeru kaavali. Quick question though...`, `Katha. Does katha
  change...`, `Mee — is Naa related to Nenu...` all scored as correct. Good.
- **Dropping an optional pronoun: never penalised.** `Sare, idhi kaavali` for "Okay, I want
  this" was accepted with no pronoun at all. Also accepted was `Nenu pusthakam kaavali`
  *with* a pronoun for a prompt that didn't ask for one — so the grader is loose in both
  directions.

And the flip side, which worries me more than the false negatives: **an outright non-answer
scored as correct.** My English "I don't think you've taught me 'am' for a person" got
"Correct!", and the incomplete `Namaskaram, naa peru` got "Exactly!". The grader looks like
it's checking for keywords, not sentences.

| 5 | Does the verb always come last? You never said that. | bundled on correct answer, after a wrong one | **Ignored** — pasted the unrelated -andi note | credit kept |
| 6 | Does *kaavali* come after the thing I want, like *undi*? | bundled on correct answer | **Answered**, properly, in English, with an example | credit kept |

The pattern: bundling a question onto a correct answer never costs you credit, which is
good. But the *usual* response is to re-print a canned grammar note, and roughly half the
time it's a note about a completely different word than the one I asked about. Only lesson 4
ever produced a real, generated answer.

**5. Did the tutor reply in Telugu, or use Telugu I was never taught?**

The tutor never conducted the lesson in Telugu — everything was in English, which was the
right call for my level. It did quote Telugu:

- `Someone asks 'Meeru ekkada?'` — both words taught. Fine, and it was a nice touch.
- *Illu ikkada undi* used in the *undi* note two steps before either word was taught. Not fine.
- *pani* → *paniki* in the -ki note. Never taught, never returned to.
- `Namaskaram, naa peru Ravi` — **Ravi** never taught.

**6. Did the tutor ever state an answer the grader rejected, or assert something that
contradicts the lesson?**

Both, once each, and the first is serious.

- **Contradictory assertion.** I asked how to say "I want" using the word for I. The tutor
  replied: *"Nenu kaavali means 'I want.'"* Two lessons later, lesson 5 teaches me **naaku**
  = "to me" and explains that Telugu builds exactly this kind of sentence with the "to me"
  form, not the plain pronoun. So lesson 4's tutor taught me a construction that lesson 5's
  own grammar note contradicts. I then tested it: I answered `Nenu pusthakam kaavali` to the
  "Book want" drill and got **"Perfect!"**, so the grader happily confirmed the bad form. As
  a learner I'd have walked away with *nenu kaavali* stuck in my head and nothing to
  dislodge it.
- **Answer leaked, then marked wrong.** In lesson 4, when I asked a question instead of
  answering, the tutor said `"Adhi oddu".` — the exact answer — and *in the same turn*
  marked me "Not quite" and re-asked the question. So it told me the answer and penalised me
  for not knowing it, simultaneously.

**7. Typos — graded consistently, correct spelling shown, no false "correction"?**

This part works well and is the most polished thing in the three lessons.

| What I typed | Target | Kind of slip | Graded | Spelling shown? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `Ikkaada` | Ikkada | vowel-length | Correct — "Good." | Yes: "Note the spelling: **Ikkada**" |
| `Kavali` | Kaavali | vowel-length (dropped two long vowels) | Correct — "Correct." | Yes: "**Kaavali**" |
| `Niru` | Neeru | transliteration slip on `nee-` | Correct — "Exactly!" | Yes: "**Neeru**" |
| `Aayana` | Ayana | followed the hint literally | Correct — "Good." | Yes: "**Ayana**" |

Consistent, forgiving, and it always showed me the right spelling. **No spelling
"correction" was ever fired on a word I'd spelled right** — every word I typed correctly
passed silently, including all six answers with English questions bolted on.

One exception, and it's not really a typo bug: `Namaskaram, naa peru` got "Note the
spelling: **Namaskaram, naa peru Ravi**", which isn't a spelling note, it's the grader using
the typo channel to reveal a hard-coded answer. And `Aayana` is arguably the app's fault —
the hint `[say it like: aa-yuh-nuh]` starts with a long *aa* for a word spelled with a
short *A*, and the same mismatch exists for `Ame` / `aa-meh`. If the hint says *aa*, learners
will type *aa*.

**8. Stray internal text?**

No `null`, `undefined`, `{w}`, `[not simulated]`, or truncated lines anywhere. Clean on
that front. But:

- **Doubled/merged pronunciation hints, twice:** `[say it like: Akkada — uhk-kuh-duh, Illu —
  ihl-lu]` and `[say it like: Ame — aa-meh, Katha — kuh-thuh]`. Two hints in one bracket, in
  a different format from every other hint, one of them for a word I wasn't asked to say.
- **Word-for-word English gloss leaking into prompts:** "Say 'Okay, water want'", "Say 'Food
  don't want'", "say 'Book want'", "say 'That don't want'". That's Telugu order written in
  English. It's a useful crutch by accident but it reads as untranslated scaffolding, and
  lesson 4 mixes both styles ("Say 'Want water'" and then "Say 'Okay, water want'") for the
  same construction.
- **Prompt/answer mismatch:** "Identify a book as 'your book'" wanting *adhi mee pusthakam*.

**9. Two tasks in one turn, or praise on a turn I never took?**

Both, several times.

- **Two things in one turn:** the Akkada+Illu turn and the Ame+Katha turn each teach two
  words plus a rule and drill one of them. The lesson-4 turn where the tutor spoke the answer
  `"Adhi oddu"` and then said "Not quite" is answer-and-rejection in the same breath.
- **Praise on a turn I never took:** "Correct!" for my English sentence saying I didn't know
  how to say "I am here". "Exactly!" for the incomplete `Namaskaram, naa peru`. And "Perfect!"
  for `Nenu pusthakam kaavali`, which lesson 5 implies is wrong.
- **Silent grading:** twice at phase boundaries (`Sare, idhi kaavali` → straight to "Phrases
  done"; `Ayana katha` → straight to "Phrases done") I got no feedback at all. Not wrong, but
  after a run of "Perfect!"/"Correct!" the silence reads as though something failed.
- **Note landing on the wrong turn:** the *Ayana*-is-respectful note arrived as feedback to
  `Naa peru`.
- **Redundant drills:** lesson 3 asked "Ask 'Where is the house?'" and then, four steps
  later, "Ask Miko where the home is" — same answer, *illu ekkada undi?*. Likewise "Say 'Go
  there'" and "Tell someone to go there" (the second only differing by wanting the polite
  *-andi*, which the prompt doesn't signal). "home" vs "house" for the same word is a small
  wobble too.

**10. Could I now use this grammar on a new word?**

- **Lesson 3's "to" ending (-ki / illu→inti): No.** This is the headline failure. The -ki
  rule was taught in a note and then **never appeared in a single drill or sentence in the
  whole lesson.** Not in vocabulary, not in the quiz, not in the sentence-building phase, not
  in the conversation phase, and not in the closing "You can now say" list. Given a new noun
  I could guess "add -ki" and would have no idea whether the stem changes, because the one
  thing the note told me is that Telugu "does that to most nouns" — and my two questions
  about which nouns and how were both ignored. A lesson titled "The 'Where' & 'Going'" that
  teaches the going-to ending and never asks you to use it hasn't taught it.
- **Lesson 4's want / don't-want: Yes, for a thing.** `X kaavali` / `X oddu` is solid; I'd
  confidently say *pusthakam kaavali* or *katha oddu* for a brand-new noun. But **"I want"
  with a person in it: no** — I was told *nenu kaavali*, which lesson 5 contradicts, and I
  was never once asked to build *naaku ... kaavali*. So I can want things impersonally and
  that's it.
- **Lesson 5's possessives: Yes, genuinely.** `naa / mee / ayana / ame` + noun drilled four
  times across four different nouns, including nouns from earlier lessons. I built *ayana
  katha* and *idhi naa katha* myself and would happily attach any of the four to a new noun.
  This is the one thing in the three lessons I'd call learned.
- **Lesson 5's *naaku* / "no verb for have": No.** Taught in the best note of the set and
  then never drilled — no exercise anywhere asked me to say "I have a book". It's in the
  closing word list as "Naaku — To me / I have", which flatters what I actually practised
  (nothing).

### If I could fix five things

1. Lesson 5's introduce-yourself drill accepting any name (and never demanding *Ravi*).
2. Move lesson 3's SOV note to before the first word-order drill, and drill *-ki* at least
   twice — that's the lesson's stated subject.
3. Split the two-words-in-one-turn steps, which also fixes the doubled hints and gives
   *Akkada* and *Ame* a drill each.
4. Give lesson 3 a person-copula, or stop asking for "I am here".
5. Kill *"Nenu kaavali means 'I want'"* and drill *naaku ... kaavali* in lesson 5.
