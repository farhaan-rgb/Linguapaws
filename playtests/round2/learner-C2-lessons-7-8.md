# Playtest — Learner C2, Telugu lessons 7 & 8

I'd just finished lessons 1–6. English is my only language. Both lessons completed in 15/15 steps,
so nothing hard-blocked me — but I got tripped up in the same place four separate times, and it was
always the same mechanical thing rather than the Telugu.

---

## What I actually learned and could use unprompted

**Lesson 7 (numbers).** Okati, Rendu, Moodu, Naalugu, Aidhu. These stuck fine — the drip-feed of
one word at a time then a three-question memory check works well. The very first thing Miko said was
also the most useful thing in the lesson:

> "One" is **Okati** on its own. Before a noun it shortens to *oka* — *oka pusthakam*, "one book" —
> the same way *idhi* becomes *ee*.

Tying it back to *idhi/ee* from lesson 2 made it click immediately, and "Oka pusthakam" came out of
me without hesitating. That's the single best-written line in either lesson.

By the end of 7 I could genuinely produce, cold: any of the five numbers standing alone, `oka +
noun`, `Rendu kaavali`, and `Naaku oka akka undi`.

**Lesson 8 (plurals).** Lu, Pusthakalu, Snehithulu, Guruvulu, Kurchilu, Chala. The `-lu` ending is
easy and I can now build it myself. `Chala pusthakalu`, `Naa snehithulu`, `Ee kurchilu oddu`,
`Pusthakalu ekkada undi?` all came out unprompted. The "have" shape carried over from lesson 7 and I
reused it without being told to.

The best explanation in either lesson was this one, and it's the answer to the question I'd have
asked a human teacher first:

> This is how Telugu says "have": not "I have a sister" but "to me one sister there is" — *naaku*
> (to me) from lesson 5, *undi* (there is) from lesson 3. Every "I have" sentence in this course is
> built this way.

## Where I got stuck

### 1. The tutor tells me I'm right, then marks me wrong — same turn

This happened three times and it is by far the worst thing in the run. Lesson 7:

> **Me:** You never showed me how to say 'have' as a full sentence, so I'm guessing by gluing pieces
> together: Naaku oka akka undi
> **Tutor:** Naaku oka akka undi is correct.  Now, try saying 'I have two friends'.
> **Tutor:** Not quite. Hint: To me + one + sister + there is. Tell Miko you have one sister.

"is correct" and "Not quite" in the same breath. Lesson 8, cleanest reproduction:

> **Me:** You've only given me 'Ee' for 'this' before a noun - never a plural 'these'. Do I need a
> different word, or is 'Ee kurchilu oddu' right?
> **Tutor:** "Ee kurchilu oddu" is right! You got it.  Now, ask 'Where are my friends?'.
> **Tutor:** Not quite. Hint: These + chairs + don't want. Say 'I don't want these chairs'.

I worked out the trigger by accident: **if I attach any English to a correct answer, the grader fails
me.** When I retyped the identical Telugu on its own line — `Naaku oka akka undi`, `Ee kurchilu oddu`
— it passed instantly. So the lesson quietly punishes asking questions, which is the opposite of
what a beginner needs. It also teaches me not to ask, which is why I stopped asking mid-lesson-8 and
started answering bare.

### 2. Lesson 7 asked me for a plural before lesson 8 taught plurals

Step: "Say 'Five houses'." I had *Illu* but no plural rule at all. I said so:

> **Me:** I know Aidhu and I know Illu = house, but I don't know how to make 'houses' plural. You
> haven't taught me that yet. Is it just 'Aidhu illu'?

The tutor confirmed `Aidhu illu`, and afterwards a note appeared explaining that after a number the
noun doesn't change. That note is exactly what I needed — **but it arrived after the drill, not
before it.** For the two turns I was actually stuck, I had no way to know. Move that note in front of
the prompt and the stumble disappears.

### 3. Lesson 7 and lesson 8 contradict each other on numbers + plurals

Lesson 7 told me, flatly:

> *Illu* did not change. After a number Telugu leaves the noun as it is — the number has already
> told you there is more than one, so the plural ending would be saying it twice.

Then lesson 8 asked for "Two friends" and the answer was `Rendu snehithulu` — with the plural
ending. I flagged the conflict directly and got the answer with no explanation, and then this note:

> Here the plural ending DOES stay, because *snehithulu* is simply the word for "friends" — that is
> the form you learned. Compare *aidhu illu* in the last lesson, where the noun was already singular
> and stayed that way.

That doesn't resolve anything for me. *Illu* was "already singular" and *snehithudu* is also already
singular — so why does one take `-lu` after a number and the other doesn't? As written the rule
reduces to "whichever form we happened to teach you," which isn't a rule I can apply to a new word.
If I meet a sixth noun tomorrow I still can't tell you whether to add `-lu` after `Rendu`. **This is
the one thing I'd fix first after the false-negative bug.**

### 4. Lesson 7 taught me a sentence lesson 8 says is wrong

Lesson 7 taught `Naaku oka akka undi` for "I have one sister" and called it correct. Lesson 8 then
told me:

> with people the verb is *unnaru*, not *undi*. Telugu keeps things and people apart here: *undi* for
> a book or a house, *unnaru* for friends and family.

*Akka* is a person. So the sentence lesson 7 drilled into me is, by lesson 8's own rule, wrong — and
it's in my notebook that way. Related: when I wrote `Naaku chala snehithulu undi` in lesson 8, using
*undi* for people, I was marked **"Exactly!"** and only then told *unnaru* was correct. So the grader
accepted a sentence the note immediately calls wrong.

### 5. Every single correction turn gave me two things to do

Four times, and it never once told me which one counted:

- "The phrase is 'Aidhu illu'.  Now, can you say **'Two cats'**?" / "So — Say 'Five houses'."
- "Rendu snehithulu.  Now, say **'My book'**?" / "So — Say 'Two friends'."
- "Naaku oka akka undi is correct.  Now, try saying **'I have two friends'**." / "Not quite. … Tell Miko you have one sister."
- "Okati, Rendu, Moodu.  Now, can you count **from four to six**?" / "Not quite. … Count 1, 2, 3."

I called it out at the time ("Wait, you just asked me two things at once") and got the same
double-barrelled reply back. Two of those bonus asks were also things I'd never been taught: **"cats"**
(no word for cat anywhere in lessons 1–8) and **"count from four to six"** (six was never taught —
the lesson stops at five). Ignoring the first half and answering the second is the only thing that
works, but a beginner has no reason to know that.

### 6. Correction turns hand me the answer

Every wrong answer, and every non-answer, was met with the target phrase printed in full. I tested
this deliberately by just typing `ok`:

> **Me:** ok
> **Tutor:** Moodu pusthakalu.  Now, can you say 'Five friends'?

One "ok" and the answer is on screen, no push-back. I finished lesson 8's last step without ever
recalling it. That's not the bracketed `(Chala pusthakalu)` style hint from the old build — the
prompts themselves were clean — but the effect on me is identical.

### 7. Typo handling is not consistent

- Lesson 7: `Mudu` for **Moodu** → rejected ("Not quite — here it is again"). Fair enough.
- Lesson 8: `Naa snehitulu` for **Naa snehithulu** (dropped the *h*) → **"Correct!"**

Same class of slip, opposite verdicts. I'd rather it were strict in both places than guess where the
line is. Notably the rejection was on a single word during the teaching phase and the acceptance was
inside a phrase, so it may just be that phrase grading is fuzzier than word grading — but I can't
see that from the learner's seat.

### 8. Internal placeholder leaked into the chat

When I asked a question during lesson 8's vocabulary phase, I got this back verbatim:

> **Tutor:** [conversation step — not simulated]

Then it re-read me the same vocabulary card. My correct answer `Chala` (I'd typed it as "Chala.
Quick question though…") was thrown away in the process.

### 9. Smaller things

- **One turn taught two new words.** "'Chairs' is **Kurchilu**. **Chala** means 'many'…" — then asked
  only for *Chala*. The pronunciation hints stacked up oddly too:
  `[say it like: Kurchilu: kur-chee-lu]  [say it like: Chala: chaa-laa]`. Every other card taught one
  word at a time and read much better for it.
- **I only ever met the plural of "chair."** *Kurchilu* was taught with no singular; *kurchi* first
  appeared inside a grammar note two steps later. Small, but I'd been told the `-lu` rule and had
  nothing to apply it to.
- **`Naaku rendu kaavali` was marked "close, but"** for "I want two." Given lesson 5 gave me *Naaku =
  to me / I have* and lesson 4 gave me *Kaavali*, the explicit version is the natural thing for a
  beginner to build. The target `Rendu kaavali` is fine, but I'd rather mine were accepted than
  corrected.
- **The `-m` never got explained.** *Pusthakam* → *Pusthakalu* loses the *m*. I asked; nothing came
  back. The later note said "*kurchi* to *kurchilu*, *guruvu* to *guruvulu*" — both of which just add
  `-lu` — so it actually made the *m* look more like an exception, not less.

## How it felt

Genuinely good in the stretches where I was just answering. The pacing is right, the notebook is
right, and three of the grammar notes (the *oka/ee* parallel, the "to me there is" note, the *ee*
doesn't-change note) are the kind of explanation I'd want from a person.

What soured it is that **asking a question is the thing that goes wrong.** Twice I did exactly what a
confused beginner should do — flagged that something hadn't been taught — and got told I was right
and marked wrong in the same message. By the middle of lesson 8 I'd learned to shut up and type bare
words, which is a worse way to learn and not what I'd have chosen. The lesson-7-vs-8 contradiction on
plurals is the other thing that would stop me: I finished lesson 8 with a `-lu` rule I can recite and
can't apply.

## The seven questions

**1. Did the tutor ever state an answer that the grader then marked wrong?**

**Yes — three times, and it's still the worst bug.** Not the old "Illulu" shape (no invented words
appeared this run; every form I was given — *Aidhu illu*, *Rendu snehithulu*, *Naaku oka akka undi*,
*Ee kurchilu oddu*, *Moodu pusthakalu* — was a real phrase). What survives is the *contradiction*:
"Naaku oka akka undi is correct." → "Not quite." and "'Ee kurchilu oddu' is right! You got it." →
"Not quite." The trigger is attaching English commentary to a correct Telugu answer — the same words
resubmitted bare pass immediately. So it's a grader/narrator mismatch rather than bad curriculum
data, and it specifically punishes learners who ask questions.

*Separately and arguably worse:* lesson 7 taught `Naaku oka akka undi` as correct, and lesson 8's
grammar note says people take *unnaru*, not *undi* — so a sentence the course taught me is wrong by
the course's own later rule.

**2. Did lesson 7 ask for plural forms before lesson 8 taught plurals?**

**Yes.** "Say 'Five houses'" in lesson 7, with no plural rule anywhere in lessons 1–6. The
justifying note ("after a number the noun does not change") exists and is good, but it fires *after*
the drill, so I was stuck for two turns first. A correction turn also asked for **"I have two
friends"** — the *snehithulu* plural — still inside lesson 7. Softer than the old version, not gone.

**3. Did any prompt hand me the answer in brackets?**

**Not in the prompts — fixed.** No "Say 'Many books' (Chala pusthakalu)" anywhere. But **every
correction turn prints the full answer**, so a single `ok` gets it handed to me, and the lesson-7
opener gives away `oka pusthakam` before asking for it. Same net effect by a different route.

**4. Did any single turn give me two different things to do?**

**Yes — all four correction turns, in both lessons, with no indication which was scored.** Listed in
full above. Two of the bonus asks were untaught content ("Two cats"; "count from four to six" when
five is the ceiling). This one is fully unfixed and I hit it every time I got something wrong.

**5. Did the tutor reply in Telugu, or use Telugu I'd never been taught?**

**No Telugu-only replies** — all instruction was English. Untaught Telugu did surface twice, both
mild: *kurchi* (singular of chair) appeared inside a grammar note without ever being taught, and
*unnaru* was introduced in lesson 8 as new (though my notebook has it from lesson 1 as "You are",
so the meaning shifted without comment). It also asked me for English concepts with no Telugu at all
behind them — "cat", "six".

**6. Was the "I have one sister" sentence learnable?**

**Yes — this is the clear win.** The note ("not 'I have a sister' but 'to me one sister there is'")
made it land, credited *naaku* to lesson 5 and *undi* to lesson 3, and I reused the pattern
unprompted in lesson 8 (`Naaku chala snehithulu undi`). The one flaw: it taught *undi* for a person,
and lesson 8 then says people need *unnaru* — so the sentence I learned is wrong and I never got
told to update it.

**7. Were typos handled consistently?**

**No.** `Mudu` → **Moodu** was rejected; `snehitulu` → **snehithulu** was accepted. Same size of
slip, opposite outcomes. Possibly single-word grading is strict and phrase grading is fuzzy, but as
a learner I can't tell where the line is.
