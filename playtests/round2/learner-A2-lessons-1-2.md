# Telugu, lessons 1 and 2 — how it actually went

Learner A2. I'd never seen a word of Telugu before this. Two lessons, 15 steps each, both finished.

## Short version

Lesson 1 was genuinely good. Lesson 2 was mostly good and then fell over badly for three turns in the middle, including replying to me in Telugu I'd never been taught, and telling me to say something ("that is a pen") that it had never taught me the words for.

## What I can actually say now, unprompted

After lesson 1 I could hold up my end of a greeting without thinking about it:

- **Namaskaram** — hello
- **Nenu bagunnanu, meeru?** — I'm fine, and you?
- **Meeru ela unnaru?** — how are you?

And by the end of the last roleplay step I strung the whole thing together myself: *Namaskaram, nenu bagunnanu, meeru ela unnaru?* That felt great. Six words in and I could do a real exchange.

After lesson 2 I can point at things and ask about them:

- **Idhi emiti?** — what's this?
- **Adhi emiti?** — what's that?
- **Idhi pusthakam** — this is a book
- **Meeru peru emiti?** — what's your name?

The word-order rule landed hard and stuck, because it was explained at exactly the moment I needed it:

> "Two orderings in one short sentence: the pointing word (*idhi*, *adhi*) comes FIRST, before the thing — and the question word *emiti* goes LAST, where English puts 'what' first."

That's the single most useful sentence either lesson said to me. I now expect "what" at the end and I'm not going to forget it.

## Where I got stuck

**Lesson 1, step 12 — "I am fine, and you?"**

I was asked to say this having never been given a word for "and". I said so out loud:

> Me: "Bagunnanu, meeru? (guessing - you never taught me a word for 'and')"
> Tutor: "Got it. The correct phrase is 'Nenu bagunnanu, meeru?'. Now, can you try again with that?"

So it turns out there *is* no word for "and" here and my guess was structurally right — I just hadn't put *nenu* in front. Being marked wrong for omitting *nenu* stung a bit, because nothing up to that point suggested it was required. And then the grammar note *after* I got it right said:

> "Telugu usually drops *nenu* — *bagunnanu* already means 'I am fine' on its own. This sentence is the exception..."

That's a fine explanation, but I only got it after being dinged. If the note had come with the prompt, or if the prompt had been phrased "**I'm** fine, and you?" with the emphasis it later used, I'd have had a fair shot. Small thing, but it's the one point in lesson 1 where I felt the grader knew something I hadn't been told.

**Lesson 2, steps 13-ish — the bad bit**

I got a bit lazy and just replied "ok" to "Tell Miko 'This is a book'". Three things went wrong at once:

> Tutor: "Idhi pusthakam. Now, tell me 'That is a pen'."
> Tutor: "Not quite. Hint: Simple statement. Tell Miko 'This is a book'."

First, it **handed me the answer** — "Idhi pusthakam" — the exact thing I was about to be scored on. Second, it then told me to say **'That is a pen'**, which is a completely different task, and I have never been taught a word for "pen". Third, in the same breath it told me the task was still 'This is a book'. Two contradictory instructions in one turn, and one of them impossible.

So I asked, like you would ask a human teacher:

> Me: "Wait, you just told me to say 'That is a pen' and then said 'This is a book' again. Which one do you want? Also I don't know the word for pen."
> Tutor: "Naku ardham kaledu. Pusthakam ante 'Idhi pusthakam'. Now, say that."

It answered my confusion **in Telugu**. Four words I had never seen — *naku*, *ardham*, *kaledu*, *ante* — with no translation. I'm a total beginner asking for help and it replied in the language I'm trying to learn. I said so:

> Me: "I have no idea what 'Naku ardham kaledu' means - you never taught me those words. Please use English."
> Tutor: "Naku ardham kaledu ante 'I don't understand'. Now, say 'Idhi pusthakam'."

It half-fixed it — it translated *naku ardham kaledu* but then used *ante* again untranslated, and I still don't know what *ante* means. I gave up asking and typed "Idhi pusthakam", which it accepted, and the lesson finished normally. So it recovered, but for three turns I was stuck against a wall being talked at in a language I don't speak. If I were a real learner alone with this app at that moment, that's where I'd have closed it.

Worth noting: the "Not quite. Hint: Simple statement." line was identical all three times. It was the *chatter above it* that kept changing and kept being wrong. If that chatter had just been silent, nothing bad would have happened.

## Things asked of me that were never taught

1. **"and"** in "I am fine, and you?" (lesson 1). Turned out not to exist as a word, but I couldn't have known.
2. **"pen"** in "Now, tell me 'That is a pen'" (lesson 2). Never taught, and this wasn't even the real task — it was stray chatter.
3. **"Combine 'You' (from last lesson) and 'Name What?'"** (lesson 2). I worked out it wanted *Meeru peru emiti?*, but the instruction is written in a weird half-code that took me a re-read. To its credit it then told me straight away that the proper form *Mee* is coming later, which stopped me feeling like I'd been taught something wrong.

## The six questions

**1. Did the tutor ever state an answer that was then marked wrong?**

No — not in the "chesa vs chesanu" sense. Both times it stated an answer, that exact answer was then accepted. In lesson 1 it said the phrase was "Nenu bagunnanu, meeru?", I typed it back verbatim, and it was marked correct. In lesson 2 it said "Idhi pusthakam", I typed it back verbatim, correct. That bug looks gone.

**2. Did the tutor ever reply to you in Telugu, or use Telugu words you'd never been taught?**

Yes, and this is the worst thing that happened. "**Naku ardham kaledu. Pusthakam ante 'Idhi pusthakam'.**" — five untaught words thrown at a beginner who had just asked a question in English. The follow-up "Naku ardham kaledu ante 'I don't understand'" still left *ante* untranslated. This only happened in the broken stretch of lesson 2; the rest of both lessons was in clean English.

**3. Did any single turn give you two different things to do?**

Yes. "Idhi pusthakam. Now, tell me 'That is a pen'." followed immediately by "Tell Miko 'This is a book'." I genuinely could not tell which was being scored, which is why I stopped and asked. Same shape again on the next turn: "...Now, say that." then "Tell Miko 'This is a book'."

Milder version, twice, in the teaching steps: one turn taught me two words at once and only drilled one — lesson 1 gave me *Ela* and *Unnaru* together, lesson 2 gave me *Peru* and *Ee* together. Both then stacked two pronunciation hints on the end (`[say it like: Peru: pay-ru] [say it like: Ee: ee]`) which reads cluttered, and it's not immediately obvious that only the last word is the one I'm supposed to type.

**4. Did the tutor ever hand you the answer in its chatter?**

Yes, once, and it made the drill a copy job: "Tell Miko 'This is a book'" → I said "ok" → it printed "Idhi pusthakam" → and then still wanted me to produce "Idhi pusthakam". I just copied it off the screen. Zero learning in that step.

The lesson 1 case was different and I'd call it fair: I gave a real wrong attempt first, it corrected me, and asked me to repeat the fixed version. That's what a teacher does. The lesson 2 case fired off a lazy "ok" with no attempt at all.

**5. Were my typos handled sensibly, and did it show me the correct spelling?**

Inconsistently, and lesson 2 is the version I want everywhere.

- Lesson 2, I typed **"Emithi"** for *Emiti*: "Yes. **It is spelled Emiti.**" Perfect — accepted, and corrected me.
- Lesson 2, I typed **"Pustakam"** for *Pusthakam*: "Good. **It is spelled Pusthakam.**" Also perfect.
- Lesson 1, I typed **"Meru"** for *Meeru*: just "Yes." No correction. I never saw that I'd got it wrong.
- Lesson 1 review, I typed **"Unaru"** for *Unnaru*: just "Exactly!" No correction.

So lesson 1 let two misspellings past silently and I'd have carried them forward. Lesson 2 caught both of mine and showed me the right spelling without making a fuss about it, which is exactly right. Whatever lesson 2's grader is doing, lesson 1 should do too.

**6. Did the pronunciation hint match the spelling I had to type?**

Mostly, with one real offender.

- **Pusthakam** → `[say it like: pus-tuh-kum]`. There's no "th" anywhere in the hint. I typed "Pustakam" straight off it and was wrong. That mismatch caused my typo.
- **Emiti** → `[say it like: ay-mih-ti]`. Fine, though "ay-" for a leading E is what pushed me toward "Emithi" by making me over-think the consonants.
- **Namaskaram** → `nuh-mus-kaa-rum`, **Nenu** → `nay-nu`, **Bagunnanu** → `baa-gun-naa-nu`, **Idhi** → `ih-dhi`, **Adhi** → `uh-dhi`, **Peru** → `pay-ru`: all clean, I typed all of these right first go.
- **Meeru** → `mee-ru` is honest, but the hint gives no way to know it's a double-e in the spelling, which is how I ended up with "Meru".

Pattern: the hints are good at vowels and unreliable at consonants. Any word with an aspirated consonant (*pusthakam*) or a doubled letter (*meeru*, *unnaru*) is a coin flip from the hint alone.

## How it felt

Pace was right. 15 steps is about one sitting, and the shape — learn six words, quick recall check, build phrases, then roleplay — makes the last stretch feel like a payoff rather than more drilling. The four checkpoint banners ("Vocabulary done", "Review passed", "Phrases done", "Scenario complete") gave me a real sense of getting somewhere.

Tone is warm without being annoying. The variety in the praise ("Correct." / "Spot on!" / "Exactly!" / "Great job!") stops it feeling robotic.

The grammar notes are the best part of this app. They arrive right when I've just used the thing, they're short, and they teach a pattern rather than a fact. *Bagunnanu* being *baga* + *unnanu* with the vowel dropping, and then the *-nu* / *-ru* / *-vu* endings carrying the person — that's a system I can now use to guess at words I haven't met. That's the difference between memorising phrases and starting to speak.

Would I keep going? After lesson 1, absolutely, no hesitation. After lesson 2 — yes, but I'd be wary. The three turns where it lectured me in Telugu while ignoring a plain English question broke the illusion that anyone was listening. Fix that one thing and I'd do lesson 3 tonight.

Two smaller wishes: let me type "I don't know" without it counting as a wrong attempt, and if I ask a question in English, answer it in English.

You: Bagunnanu, meeru? (guessing - you never taught me a word for 'and')

You: Nenu bagunnanu, meeru?
System: 💡 Telugu usually drops *nenu* — *bagunnanu* already means "I am fine" on its own. This sentence is the exception: setting yourself beside the other person is exactly the contrast Telugu marks with the pronoun, so *Nenu bagunnanu, meeru?* is the natural form here — "**I'm** fine, and you?".
System: 🎓 **Phrases done** — now real conversation.
Tutor: A shopkeeper greets you. Say hello and that you are fine.
You: Namaskaram, bagunnanu
System: 💡 Swap that -nu ending and the subject changes: *bagunnaru* means "you are fine". Same word, different ending, different person — you will meet this pattern on every Telugu verb.
Tutor: Exactly! Ask Miko how she is doing.
You: Meeru ela unnaru?
System: 💡 Leading with the name — *Miko, meeru ela...* — is as normal in Telugu as in English. Ending with *unnaru* gives you the polite full form.
Tutor: Great job! Someone asks how you are. Answer: 'I am fine'.
You: Bagunnanu
System: 💡 In relaxed speech people clip this to *bagunna*, dropping the -nu. You will hear both, and either is accepted here.
Tutor: Perfect! Say hello, tell them you are fine, and ask how they are.
You: Namaskaram, nenu bagunnanu, meeru ela unnaru?
System: 💡 That is the whole greeting exchange in one go — greeting, how you are, and the question back. *Unnaru* on the end is the polite "are"; you will be understood with or without it.
System: ✨ **Scenario complete.**
