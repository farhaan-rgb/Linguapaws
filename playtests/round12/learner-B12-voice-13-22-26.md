# Learner B12 — voice round, Telugu lessons 13, 22, 26

I listened to three lessons the way you'd listen to a podcast — audio in my ears, phone in my pocket,
glancing at the screen maybe once every three lines. Here's what actually happened.

---

## 9. Does this feel like something trying to teach me, or a quiz that moves on?

**Both, and it flips depending on which half of the lesson I'm in — which is the worst possible answer.**

The first thing I heard in lesson 13 was genuinely a teacher:

> "I did is Chesanu. Look at the shape: a stem, then -anu for the past, then nothing else — the -nu on
> the end is the 'I', the same -nu you met in chestunnanu. So the past is stem + -anu, and this lesson's
> five words are all built that way."

That's a person explaining a system. I sat up. And the tips that fire after a correct sentence are the
same quality — the one about "help" in lesson 22 taught me something I could immediately reuse:

> "Telugu has no single verb 'to help'. It says 'do help' — sahaayam, the noun, plus cheyagalanu, 'I can
> do'. The same join builds pani cheyagalanu, 'I can do work', out of words you already have."

But the moment I got something **wrong**, the teacher vanished and a buzzer took over. In the recall
phase of lesson 22 I answered "Cheyagalanu" — *I can do* — to the prompt "What's the word for I can't
do?". That is the single most instructive mistake available in that lesson. It's the whole lesson,
inverted. All I heard was:

> "Not quite! What's the word for 'I can't do'?"

That's it. No "you gave me the *can* ending, you want *-lenu*." No correction. Not even the right answer.
I tried again wrong on purpose, and got the identical line. In lesson 13 I answered "Tintunnanu" (present
tense) where "Thinnanu" (past) was wanted — again the exact confusion the lesson exists to fix — and
again: "Not quite!" Twice. I had to literally type *"I don't remember. Can you tell me the answer?"*
before it would tell me.

So the honest verdict: **it teaches when I'm right and quizzes when I'm wrong.** Which is backwards. The
moment I need teaching is the moment it stops.

There's a second, sharper reason it feels like a quiz: it doesn't actually check the rule it's teaching.
In lesson 26 — the whole lesson is *feeling nouns take -ga* — it asked "What's the word for Anger?" and I
deliberately said **"Kopamga"**, the -ga form, which is *angry*, not *anger*. It said **"Great job!"** The
one distinction the entire lesson rests on, and it waved it through. In lesson 13 I said **"Nenu illu
vellanu"** for "I went home" using the bare noun *illu* rather than *intiki*, and got **"Perfect!"** If
the marking doesn't care about the rule, the rule isn't really being taught — it's being narrated.

## 2. Could I apply a rule I only HEARD?

**Mostly no, and the one clean success was the exception.** This is the part I'd want you to read twice.

**The successes (2 of them, both real):**

Lesson 22 — I heard, unprompted, the "do help" explanation quoted above, including *pani cheyagalanu*.
Two steps later, when asked for "I can help", I produced **"Sahaayam cheyagalanu"** cold. That worked,
and the audio is unambiguously what enabled it — I never saw that pattern on a prompt line.

Lesson 26 — I heard "Unnara turns 'you are' into a question… Telugu adds that -a to make a question
rather than reordering the words," and separately heard the -ga frame. When asked "Are you angry?" I
built **"Meeru kopamga unnara"** from three heard pieces and one notebook word. Correct first try. That's
the app working exactly as intended.

**The failures, which outnumber them:**

**(a) I tried three times to apply a heard rule out loud, and got a non-answer every time.**

In lesson 13 the audio told me: *"Change that -nu and the subject changes with it: chesaru is 'you did',
chesadu is 'he did'."* That's an extension rule. So I tested myself out loud:

> Me: "If -du is he, would 'he went' be velladu? And 'you ate' be thinnaru?"
> Tutor: *[replays a random tip from step 2 about stems]* "That's the scenario done — nothing left to
> answer here."

No answer. I still don't know if velladu is a word.

Lesson 26, same thing:

> Me: "Testing myself here - from the -ga rule, would bhayamga mean I feel scared? You never gave me
> that one."
> Tutor: "Bhayam. 🐾 **Ikkada** means 'here'."

That's not an answer, that's a fragment plus a word from lesson 3 arriving out of nowhere. It sounded
broken.

And lesson 26 again, when I challenged an actual contradiction:

> Me: "You told me naaku X-ga undi works with every feeling. Why was naaku santhoshamga undi wrong?"
> Tutor: "Santhosham is 'happiness'. For *naaku badha ledu*, you use the bare noun because you are
> denying the feeling."

That answers a question I did not ask. **Three out of three attempts to stress-test a spoken rule got
nothing back.** The one channel where I could have proven I'd learned the pattern is the one channel
that doesn't work.

**(b) The one rule the audio told me I could rely on, the app itself rejects.**

This is the worst thing I found. Lesson 26, spoken, verbatim:

> "Two frames, and a default you can rely on. Use naaku X-ga undi — 'to me, tiredly, it is' — and **you
> will be right with every feeling here**: naaku alupuga undi, naaku kopamga undi, naaku badhaga undi."

Two steps later, asked for "I am happy", I did precisely that — **"Naaku santhoshamga undi"** — and was
marked **wrong**. When I glanced at the screen I found the sentence the audio had cut off: *"Nenu X-ga
unnanu … is the usual choice with santhosham; both are accepted."* Both are **not** accepted. The screen
half is wrong too, but at least it warns you. The audio actively misled me and then penalised me for
believing it.

**(c) When I was honestly stuck, saying so didn't help.**

Lesson 22 opened with *"Galanu is the ending for 'I can'."* — an ending on **what**? I said so plainly:

> Me: "You said Galanu is an ending - an ending on what? Where do I stick it?"

That one it answered well, and gave me *cheyagalanu* / *cheyalenu*. But I had to ask. And in the same
lesson, when I asked why "speak" is *matlada-* here when lesson 13 taught me *cheppanu*, I got:

> "Good question — that one is in your notes already, so have another look there and ask again if it is
> still unclear."

**It is not in my notes.** I checked. *Matlada* appears nowhere before this lesson. That's a brush-off
dressed as guidance, and it sent me to look at nothing.

**Net:** after three lessons of *listening*, I can extend a pattern maybe a third of the time, and I have
no way to check whether I did it right.

## 1. Did I understand WHY my answer was right or wrong, from the audio alone?

**Right answers: often yes. Wrong answers: essentially never.**

When I was right, the audio frequently told me why the shape works, and those were good:

> "Put cheyagalanu and cheyalenu side by side and you can see the join: cheya- is the stem, then -galanu
> for can and -lenu for can't."

> "Mariyu from lesson 10 joins the two verbs, and nenu need not be repeated — the -nu on each verb
> already says who did it."

Both landed. I understood them on hearing, no rewind needed.

When I was wrong, there are two different behaviours and only one of them is acceptable:

- **In the recall phase — nothing.** Three separate wrong answers across two lessons, three identical
  "Not quite!" lines. No reason, no correction, no answer.
- **In the sentence-building phase — a short hint, spoken.** "Not quite. Hint: To me + sadness + not."
  That's better. It got me to the right answer on the next try. But notice it's a *word order recipe*,
  not a reason. I produced "Naaku badha ledu" by assembling the hint, and to this day nobody has told me
  **aloud** why it's *naaku* and not *nenu* there. That explanation exists — I found it on the screen —
  but I "learned" that sentence by pattern-matching a hint, not by understanding.

So on the central question of the round: the spoken feedback explains **success** well and **failure**
barely at all, and the inconsistency between the two phases is itself confusing — I couldn't predict
whether a mistake would get me help.

## 3. Did the voice rush me to the next task before the feedback landed?

**Not usually — the pacing is fine.** The verdict, the tip and the next instruction arrive as one block
and I could follow it. Nothing overlapped, nothing got stepped on.

Two exceptions worth naming:

- **The final step of lesson 13** rushed in the sense that the tip fired *before* I'd been told I was
  right, so for a second I thought I'd made a mistake. (More on ordering in 7.)
- **After a wrong answer in the recall phase**, "Not quite!" and the re-ask are effectively one breath.
  It doesn't rush past feedback — there's no feedback to rush past. It rushes past the *opportunity* for
  feedback, which is worse.

---

## 4. Was the spoken feedback the right length?

**Never tedious. Repeatedly cut off mid-rule — and always at the same place.**

Not one moment in three lessons felt too long. The longest tips were the best ones. Length is not the
problem.

Truncation is, and it is systematic: **the spoken tip is the first sentence, and the caveat is silent.**
Three clean examples, all from comparing what I heard against what I saw when I glanced:

| Heard | Silent (screen only) |
|---|---|
| "-galanu and -lenu are endings, not words… nenu in front is optional — both are accepted here." | "What the endings will NOT let you do is build a new verb: the stem changes shape before them, so meet each pair as a pair." |
| "cheya- is the stem, then -galanu for can and -lenu for can't." | "Note the stem is *cheya*, not the *cheyu* you might expect — Telugu reshapes the verb before an ending, so learn these as pairs rather than trying to build them." |
| "Use naaku X-ga undi… you will be right with every feeling here." | The **entire** second frame, **the negation exception**, and **the third-person form**. |

In every case the audio keeps the easy half and drops the half that stops you making mistakes. The
lesson-26 one is not a trim, it's an amputation — roughly two-thirds of the rule, including both
exceptions, in a lesson whose *entire subject* is which frame a feeling takes.

One more: when a tip gets **replayed** (it happened twice when I asked a question), the replay is
truncated even when the original was spoken whole. Lesson 13's stem warning ended, live, with *"the
ENDING is what transfers"* — the actual takeaway. The replay stopped one clause short of it.

## 5. Anything valuable that was ONLY on screen?

**Yes, and it's the same list as above — the exceptions.** Everything in the right-hand column of that
table I got *only because I happened to glance*. If I'd been walking, I would have finished lesson 26
believing:

- that *naaku X-ga undi* works for every feeling (it doesn't — the app rejects it for happiness),
- with no idea that *nenu X-ga unnanu* exists,
- and with only a single passing mention, ten steps earlier at step 1, that denial behaves differently.

Also screen-only: **the concrete example of the third-person frame.** I asked out loud how to talk about
someone else's feelings. The reply I *heard* was "Unnaru is doing a second job here… it is also the
respectful 'he is / she is'." Useful. But it stopped right before *"meeru … unnaru, ayana … unnaru"* — the
bit with the actual pattern in it. I could still assemble "Ayana badhaga unnaru" because *ayana* is in my
notebook, but I had the screen open at that moment and I honestly can't swear I'd have got there blind.

## 6. Did I know where I was in the lesson when the format changed?

**Yes. This is the clearest win of the round.** Every phase change was announced aloud, plainly:

> "Vocabulary done — 5 words. Quick memory check next."
> "Review passed — now let's build whole sentences."
> "Phrases done — now real conversation."
> "Scenario complete."

Short, unambiguous, and I always knew whether the next thing was a recall or a build. No complaints at
all. Keep this exactly as it is.

## 7. Order of the turn — verdict, explanation, next instruction

**Right sequence most of the time, and audibly wrong three times.** The normal, correct shape:

> "Perfect!" → tip → "Say 'I can't do'."

That's the right order and it works. But three turns inverted it and put the **explanation before the
verdict**:

- Lesson 13, final step: tip about *mariyu*, *then* "Correct! That's the last one."
- Lesson 22, "Telugu matladagalanu": tip about the language name, *then* "Spot on!"
- Lesson 22, my combined answer + question: the deflection, *then* "Correct."

Heard rather than read, this is genuinely disorienting. I've just answered, and instead of a yes or a no
I get a paragraph — so I spend the whole paragraph assuming I've been corrected, and only find out at the
end that I was right. Verdict first, always. I need to know which emotion to have before you explain.

Related ordering bug: in lesson 22 I answered *and* asked a question in one breath. It answered the
question first, then said "Correct." — but the answer it gave was the brush-off, so the sequence I heard
was *deflection → verdict → phase-change → new question*, four things, and I lost track of which one was
about me.

## 8. Anything spoken that shouldn't be?

A few things, none catastrophic but all cheap to fix:

1. **The clipboard emoji is in the spoken line.** The end-of-lesson summary reads
   `🔊 Spoken: 📋 You can now say: …` in all three lessons. Every other spoken line has its emoji and
   markdown stripped — this one doesn't. Either "clipboard" gets read aloud or a symbol gets mangled;
   either way it's the last thing I hear in every lesson.
2. **The summary itself is a list read as prose.** "Chesanu — I did · Vellanu — I went · Thinnanu — I ate
   · Chusanu — I saw · Cheppanu — I spoke / I said" is six items with interpuncts between them. As audio
   it's a run-on. It's the natural place for a slower, spaced recap and instead it's the fastest line in
   the lesson.
3. **A bare fragment.** Lesson 26: "Bhayam. 🐾 Ikkada means 'here'." Two disconnected fragments, one of
   them a word from lesson 3 with no context, in answer to a question about something else. That would
   sound like a glitch.
4. **The pronunciation guides are fine** — "say it like: chay-saa-nu" — and I didn't hear any doubled.
   When two words came at once they were labelled ("Alupu = …", "Bhayam = …"), which was right.
5. **Asterisks and bold don't appear to be read** in the tips — the `🔊 Spoken` lines have them stripped.
   Good. The one exception is the tutor's own lines, which still carry `**Chesanu**` and `🐾`; I'll
   assume those are stripped downstream, but the summary line proves that assumption isn't safe
   everywhere.

## 10. After three lessons, what could I produce unprompted — and did I learn it by ear or by eye?

**By ear, confidently:**
- The five past-tense verbs and that the past is *stem + -anu* — *chesanu, vellanu, thinnanu, chusanu,
  cheppanu*. I could also tell you the stems are unpredictable and must be memorised individually,
  because that was said out loud and well.
- *-galanu* = can, *-lenu* = can't, and the *cheya-* pair.
- **"Sahaayam cheyagalanu"** and, by extension, *"pani cheyagalanu"* — genuinely built from audio.
- The question rule: add *-a*. I'd try *unnara* on anything. **"Meeru kopamga unnara"** came out clean.
- Feeling nouns + *-ga* to feel them.
- Combining with old vocabulary: **"Eeroju naaku chala alupuga undi"**, first try. That felt great.

**By eye only, and I'd have missed it walking:**
- That *nenu X-ga unnanu* exists at all, and that it's required for happiness.
- That denying drops the *-ga* — I heard this once, in the very first sentence of the lesson, and then
  never again until I read it.
- That the stem reshapes before *-galanu* / *-lenu*, so you can't build new ones.

**Learned wrong, by ear:** that *naaku X-ga undi* is safe for every feeling. The audio said it in those
words. It isn't true.

**Still don't know, having asked:** whether *velladu* / *thinnaru* are real words; why *matlada-* and
*cheppa-* are both "speak".

**The one-line summary.** The change is real and it's an improvement — six or seven things that used to
be silent text I now genuinely know by ear, and the phase announcements are excellent. But the voice
speaks the rule and swallows the exception, and it goes quiet at exactly the moment I get something
wrong. Right now it sounds like a good teacher who stops talking whenever you need them.

**If you fix two things, fix these:** say something real when I'm wrong in the recall phase, and stop
truncating tips before the caveat. Both are the same underlying bug from where I'm sitting — the audio
keeps the pleasant half and drops the hard half.
