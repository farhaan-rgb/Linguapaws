# Learner A12 — voice playtest, Telugu, lessons 1 / 9 / 16

Round 12. Three fresh sessions (`r12a1`, `r12a9`, `r12a16`), played in order, listening the way I'd listen to a podcast. I glanced at the screen maybe every third line. Everything below is what I actually heard unless I say otherwise.

---

## 9. Does this feel like it's trying to teach me, or like a quiz that moves on? (leading with this, as asked)

**It's a quiz that has learned to talk.** It's better than a quiz that says nothing — genuinely, it is — but the talking happens in the wrong places, so the shape of the thing hasn't changed.

Here's the tell. Across three lessons, every single time I got something **wrong**, this is the complete audio I received:

> 🔊 "Not quite! What's the word for 'Bad'?"

> 🔊 "Not quite. Hint: You + how + are. Ask 'How are you?'."

> 🔊 "Not quite. Hint: This + big + book. Tell Miko 'This is a big book'."

That's it. Three wrong answers, three lessons, zero explanation of what I actually said or why it was wrong. Meanwhile every time I got something **right**, I got a paragraph:

> 🔊 "Swap that -nu ending and the subject changes: bagunnaru means 'you are fine'. Same word, different ending, different person — you will meet this pattern on every Telugu verb."

So the app talks to me when I don't need it and goes quiet when I do. That is the exact profile of a quiz: it explains the answer key after you've already produced the answer. A teacher does it the other way round.

And when I asked for the thing I was missing, it knew it perfectly well and had just been withholding it. Lesson 9:

> You: "Not quite doesn't help me. What did I actually say? Was Chinna wrong because it means something else?"
> 🔊 Tutor: "'Chinna' means 'small' or 'little.' The word for 'Bad' is 'Chedu.'"

One sentence. That's all I ever wanted. It was sitting right there and I had to fight for it. If that sentence had been spoken automatically the first time, this would feel like a tutor.

Two more things push it towards "quiz". First, three times across the run I asked a real question and got fobbed off:

> 🔊 "Good question — that one is in your notes already, so have another look there and ask again if it is still unclear."

I am listening to this while walking. I cannot "have another look at my notes." And in lesson 16 it was flatly untrue — I'd asked whether `-ga` on baga/tvaraga/mellaga was a pattern, it told me to check my notes, and then **the very next sentence out of its mouth** taught me the -ga rule:

> 🔊 "Santhosham is 'happiness'. To say you ARE happy, add -ga: santhoshamga, and pair it with unnanu — nenu santhoshamga unnanu."

It knew. It just wouldn't answer a question that wasn't on its script.

Second, the whole middle stage of lesson 9 — the sentence-building stage, three tasks in a row — produced this and nothing else:

> 🔊 "Perfect!" … 🔊 "Correct!" … 🔊 "Spot on!"

Nobody ever told me adjectives go before nouns in Telugu. I guessed it from English and got lucky three times, and the app congratulated me three times for a rule it never stated.

**Verdict: the spoken feedback is a real improvement and I'd keep it. But it is currently a reward, not a teaching tool, and until wrong answers get explained out loud this is still a quiz that moves on.**

## 1. Did I understand WHY my answer was right or wrong, from the audio alone?

**Right answers: often yes, and some of it was genuinely good. Wrong answers: never. Not once.**

The good side first, because it's real. This, heard in lesson 9 at the moment the word was introduced, is the best thing in the whole run:

> 🔊 "'Hot' is Vedi. Bagundi is 'it is good' — baga ('well') joined onto undi ('it is'), the same joining you met in bagunnanu."

That landed. It reached back to a word I'd learned in lesson 1 and showed me the machinery. I understood *bagundi* rather than memorising it.

And this one, lesson 16:

> 🔊 "Baga sits right before the verb, not at the front of the sentence like English puts 'well' at the end."

Clumsily worded — I'm still not sure what the English half of that sentence is claiming — but "right before the verb" is a rule, spoken, that I could use.

The wrong side. Three errors, three lessons:

- Lesson 1, I answered "Meeru" when asked for "You are". Heard: **"Not quite!"** and the identical question again. I didn't know whether Meeru was a different word, a wrong form, or just misheard.
- Lesson 9, I answered "Chinna" for "Bad". Heard: **"Not quite!"**. It never told me *chinna* means "small" until I demanded it.
- Lesson 9, I said "Ee pedda pusthakam" for "This is a big book". Heard: **"Not quite. Hint: This + big + book."** — which is useless, because I *did* say this + big + book. My error was picking the wrong "this". The hint was blind to what I'd actually done. I asked directly why *Ee* was wrong and got the notes brush-off. **I got the sentence right on the next try by swapping in a word, and to this moment I still don't know the difference between Ee and Idhi.** I passed the lesson without learning the thing the lesson was testing.

There's also a worse case than silence — feedback that actively misled me. Lesson 1, I asked where "how" goes in the sentence:

> 🔊 "In Telugu, the order is: how + you + are. for 'How are you?'."

So I said "Ela Meeru Unnaru". And got:

> 🔊 "Not quite. Hint: You + how + are."

It contradicted itself in two turns. When I called it out:

> 🔊 "That was a misunderstanding. in a polite way."

That is not a sentence. Heard aloud it is gibberish. I had no idea whether I'd been wrong, it had been wrong, or something had broken.

## 2. Did the voice rush me to the next task before I'd absorbed the feedback?

Yes, and there's a structural reason: **several explanations arrive before the verdict, so I'm listening to a rule while still not knowing whether I passed.** Lesson 9's last step, heard in this order:

> 🔊 "Nothing between the two words — adhi chedu, and likewise idhi manchi for 'this is good'."
> 🔊 "Correct! That's the last one."

I spent the whole explanation half-listening because I was waiting to find out if I'd got it right. Same thing in lesson 1 twice, including the worst instance:

> 🔊 "Unnaru is the verb 'are': unnanu is 'I am' … and unnaru is 'you are' for anyone you would call meeru — and the same form doubles as the respectful 'he is / she is / they are', so listen for who is being talked about."
> 🔊 "Not quite. Hint: You + how + are. Ask 'How are you?'."

A forty-word grammar lecture, and only at the end of it do I learn I was wrong — and it's wrong about *word order*, which the lecture said nothing about. Wrong explanation, wrong place, and it delayed the thing I needed.

The other rush is at lesson end. "Correct! That's the last one" → "Scenario complete" → the whole six-word summary list, in one uninterrupted block. No pause to let the last explanation settle.

Where it was fine: the mid-lesson explanations that came *after* "Perfect!" and before the next instruction gave me a natural beat. That ordering works (see Q7).

## 3. Was the spoken feedback the right length?

Mostly yes for the praise-explanations — two sentences, about right, I stayed with them. Nothing was tedious; I never zoned out from length.

The problem is the opposite one, and it's severe: **things are cut off mid-rule.** The spoken version is repeatedly a truncated prefix of what's on screen, and the truncation lands right before the point.

Lesson 9, the single most useful explanation in the run. On screen:

> "Nothing between the two words — adhi chedu, and likewise idhi manchi for 'this is good'. Telugu has two different jobs here and keeps them apart: undi says a thing EXISTS or is somewhere (illu akkada undi), and bagundi has it built in for exactly that reason. But pinning a plain description onto a noun needs no verb at all, which is why adhi chedu is already a complete sentence."

What I heard:

> 🔊 "Nothing between the two words — adhi chedu, and likewise idhi manchi for 'this is good'."

The rule — *a description on a noun needs no verb* — and the undi-vs-bagundi distinction, which is a thing I would genuinely have got wrong later, were both cut. I only know they exist because I happened to be looking at the screen at that moment.

Lesson 1, "I am fine, and you?". What I heard:

> 🔊 "Telugu usually drops nenu — bagunnanu already means 'I am fine' on its own. This sentence is the exception: setting yourself beside the other person is exactly the contrast Telugu marks with the pronoun, so Nenu bagunnanu, meeru?"

It stops on "so Nenu bagunnanu, meeru?" and just… ends. Heard aloud that's a dangling fragment — the "so" sets up a conclusion that never comes. On screen the sentence finishes with the gloss "— 'I'm fine, and you?'". Out loud it sounds like the tutor lost its train of thought.

Lesson 1, unnaru: the spoken version dropped the final sentence "The ending carries the person, so it shifts with who you mean: a close friend gets unnavu." So the *unnavu* form exists on screen only.

So: not too long, not too short — **amputated**. Three times I heard the start of a rule and not the end.

## 5. Did I know where I was in the lesson?

**Yes, and this is the change that clearly works.** All three stage transitions are spoken, in all three lessons:

> 🔊 "Vocabulary done — 6 words. Quick memory check next."
> 🔊 "Review passed — now let's build whole sentences."
> 🔊 "Phrases done — now real conversation."
> 🔊 "Scenario complete."

Short, clear, told me both what just ended and what's coming. I always knew whether I was being taught, tested, or asked to build. No notes, keep it exactly as is. This is the unambiguous win of the round.

## 6. Did hearing an explanation let me get a later answer right?

**Yes — three times, and this is the strongest evidence the change is worth keeping.**

**Case 1, the clearest.** Lesson 16, after "I am doing well", I heard:

> 🔊 "Baga sits right before the verb, not at the front of the sentence like English puts 'well' at the end."

Next task was "Go slowly". I said **"Mellaga vellandi"** and got it first try. Without that line I'd have put the adverb after the verb — English word order — and guessed. I placed it on a rule I had heard thirty seconds earlier, not on luck.

**Case 2.** Lesson 16, the -ga explanation (the one it had just refused to give me, then gave anyway):

> 🔊 "To say you ARE happy, add -ga: santhoshamga, and pair it with unnanu — nenu santhoshamga unnanu."

Four steps later: "Say 'I am very happy'." I said **"Nenu chala santhoshamga unnanu"**. Without hearing that, I'd have said "Nenu chala santhosham" — happiness, not happy — and been told "Not quite" with no idea why.

**Case 3, across lessons.** Lesson 1, on *unnaru*:

> 🔊 "…the same form doubles as the respectful 'he is / she is / they are', so listen for who is being talked about."

Fifteen lessons later, lesson 16, "Ask 'How is your friend?'" — I said **"Mee snehithudu ela unnaru?"** confidently, because I'd been told *unnaru* covers a third person spoken of respectfully. That is a rule surviving from audio across two sessions and paying off. That's real learning.

So the mechanism works. It works entirely on the praise path, though — every one of these came after a correct answer. It has never once helped me recover from a mistake, because on the mistake path nothing is spoken.

## 7. Order of the turn — verdict, explanation, next instruction

**When it actually goes verdict → explanation → instruction, it is exactly right.** I get closure, then the reason, then the new job. Nothing to change:

> 🔊 "Correct!"
> 🔊 "The -andi ending from lesson 3 again — vellandi rather than vellu is the polite way to tell someone to do something. The adverb leads: mellaga vellandi, 'slowly go'."
> 🔊 "Say 'I am doing well'."

**But it doesn't always go that way.** At least four times the explanation came out *first*, before the verdict — lesson 1 twice, lesson 9's last step, lesson 16's -ga. Heard aloud, that's genuinely disorienting: I'm being lectured about a sentence and I don't yet know if it was mine or a correction of mine. Fix the ordering to be consistent and this is solved.

One change I'd want on top: **for wrong answers, put a one-line explanation between the verdict and the retry.** "Not quite — chinna means small; bad is chedu. What's the word for 'Bad'?" Verdict, reason, retry. Same shape, applied to the half where it's missing.

## 8. Anything spoken that shouldn't be?

Several. Some of these would sound plainly broken out loud.

- **"That was a misunderstanding. in a polite way."** (lesson 1). Two fragments welded together with a full stop in the middle. There's no reading of this that makes sense aloud.
- **"In Telugu, the order is: how + you + are. for 'How are you?'."** (lesson 1). Full stop mid-sentence again, then a trailing prepositional phrase. And a spoken colon-plus-plus-signs — how does "how plus you plus are" even get read?
- **The summary line is read with its emoji and its separators**: 🔊 "📋 You can now say: Namaskaram — Hello · Nenu — I · Meeru — You · …". The clipboard emoji is in the *spoken* string, and there are six middot separators. That's either six read-out symbols or six odd pauses. The stage announcements correctly strip their emoji ("🎓 **Vocabulary done**" → 🔊 "Vocabulary done") — the summary line doesn't.
- **Markdown in the teaching lines.** The tutor's own turns still carry raw `**` and `*`: 🔊 "To say 'Hello', say **Namaskaram**", 🔊 "the polite form, for anyone you would call *Meeru*". Only the 💡 blocks get cleaned up. If the 💡 path strips them, the tutor path should too.
- **Dangling fragments used as examples.** 🔊 "Leading with the name — Miko, meeru ela... — is as normal in Telugu as in English." An ellipsis mid-sentence, spoken, is just a sentence that trails off and restarts.
- **Verbatim repetition.** In lesson 16 I heard "Baga sits right before the verb…" twice, word for word, two turns apart — the second time in place of an answer to a question I'd asked. Same lesson, the santhosham/-ga explanation was spoken twice in consecutive turns. Hearing the identical paragraph twice in a row makes it sound like the app has hung.
- **"Note the spelling"** — 🔊 "Correct. Note the spelling: **Vedi Bagundi**." I'm *speaking*. Telling a voice learner to note the spelling of a word they said out loud correctly is nonsense; it belongs on screen if anywhere.

## 10. After three lessons, what can I say in Telugu unprompted — and did I learn it by ear or by eye?

Unprompted, walking away, I can say:

- **Namaskaram, nenu bagunnanu, meeru ela unnaru?** — full greeting exchange. **By ear.**
- **Bagunnanu** on its own as an answer, and I know **bagunna** is the clipped version I'll hear in real speech. **By ear** — that was a spoken 💡.
- **Mellaga vellandi** / **tvaraga vellandi**, and I could build "do it slowly" — **by ear**, off the "baga sits right before the verb" rule.
- **Nenu chala santhoshamga unnanu** — **by ear**, off the -ga explanation.
- **Idhi ela undi?** — **by ear**, and I know it works for food, a film, a day, because it said so.
- **Adhi chedu / idhi manchi** — I can say them, **by ear**, but I'd say them *nervously*, because the reason they need no verb was cut from the audio. I only trust them because I glanced at the screen.

What I still can't do, after passing all three lessons:

- **Tell you when to use *Ee* and when to use *Idhi*.** I got marked wrong for it, asked twice, and was sent to my notes both times.
- **Say "and".** It set me a task requiring it, refused to teach it, and made me attempt the sentence anyway.
- **Use *unnavu*** — the familiar form. It exists on screen only; I'd never have heard of it.

Net: the audio taught me most of what I can now say, which two rounds ago would not have been true. But the two things I *couldn't* do are both things I asked about and was refused — and the pattern is that this app will happily explain what I already got right and will not explain what I got wrong.
