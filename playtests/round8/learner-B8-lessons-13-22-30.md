# Learner B8 — Telugu, lessons 13, 22 and 30

Round 8. Fresh sessions `r8b13`, `r8b22`, `r8b30`. I speak only English; everything I knew going in came
from the `--notes` notebook (lessons 1–12 for L13, 1–21 for L22, 1–29 for L30).

---

## How it actually went

### Lesson 13 — Simple Past Tense

This was the best-taught of the three. The very first line told me the rule *and* the shape: "a stem, then
**-anu** for the past... the -nu on the end is the 'I', the same -nu you met in *chestunnanu*." I'd met
`chestunnanu` in lesson 11, so that landed. Then the second step immediately walked it back in the most
useful way possible: "the STEM changes unpredictably... there is no rule that gets you from one to the
other. Learn each verb's stem as its own word; the ENDING is what transfers." That's honest, and it saved
me from an hour of wrong guessing. Best teaching I've had in this course.

Sentence-building was fair. I guessed `Intiki vellanu` for "I went home" by gluing `intiki` from lesson 3
onto `vellanu`, and it worked. `Ninna adhi chesanu` for "I did that yesterday" worked. Nothing asked of me
that I hadn't been given, except one soft spot: "Confirm you saw Miko" — I genuinely didn't know whether
to say "I saw" or "I saw Miko", because nothing had ever taught me how to attach a name as an object. I
asked, and it answered, but the answer was two bare Telugu lines with no explanation:

> Tutor: Miko chusanu. (I saw Miko.) Nenu Miko chusanu. (I saw Miko.)

So I copied it without understanding why no object marker is needed. And then the re-ask read badly:
"So — Confirm you saw Miko." with the capital C stuck mid-sentence.

**Where I got stuck / annoyed:** I asked twice whether I could build a new past-tense verb myself. Once it
answered well; once it was ignored entirely (details in Q3 below).

### Lesson 22 — Can & Can't

Clean, short, and it kept its promises. It taught me `galanu`/`lenu` as endings, then handed me two fixed
pairs (`cheyagalanu`/`cheyalenu`) and — crucially — told me not to trust the pattern:

> *-galanu* and *-lenu* are endings, not words. The "I" is already inside them, so *cheyagalanu* on its own
> is a complete "I can do" and *nenu* in front is optional — both are accepted here. What the endings will
> NOT let you do is build a new verb: the stem changes shape before them, so meet each pair as a pair.

Then it proved it: "Note the stem is *cheya*, not the *cheyu* you might expect." That's the second time
this course has been straight with me about a limit instead of pretending a rule is productive. I liked it.

`Sahaayam cheyagalanu` for "I can help" was a guess I flagged as a guess, and it was right, and the note
that followed explained *why* ("Telugu has no single verb 'to help'. It says 'do help'") and threw in
`pani cheyagalanu` as a bonus. That's a good note.

**Where I got stuck:** it asked me to say "I can speak Telugu" and I had never been given a word for
"Telugu". I said so in plain English — and it marked me **"Spot on!"** anyway (see Q2). I never actually
produced the sentence.

**Where it annoyed me:** three of my four questions in this lesson were swallowed. The worst was:

> You: Eeroju cheyalenu — how would I say 'he can't do it'? Does the ending change like it did in lesson 13?
> Tutor: Eeroju nenu cheyalenu. (I can't do it today.)

That's not an answer to anything I asked. It just restated my own correct sentence with `nenu` inserted —
after the lesson had explicitly told me `nenu` was optional. I still don't know how to say "he can't".

### Lesson 30 — the final lesson

**Blunt verdict: it ends, but it doesn't arrive.**

The opening line of the last lesson of a thirty-lesson course is:

> Hey there! I'm Miko, your friendly guide. 🐾 To say "I grew up", say **Perigaanu**

Word-for-word the same cold introduction lesson 13 gave me. Miko introduces herself to me on lesson 30. No
"this is the last one", no "thirty lessons ago you couldn't say hello". I only learned this was the finale
sideways, from a note about `malli` — "which is what you want at the end of a course" — four steps in. I
asked outright, "is this actually the last lesson of the whole course?", and got two notes, neither of
which said yes; the closest was "that is the point of a capstone", which I had to infer from.

The middle of the lesson is where it comes good. The callbacks are real and they're specific:

- `nundi`: "Pair it with *dhanyavaadaalu* from lesson 10 and you can close any conversation."
- `matladagalanu`: "the *matladu* + *-galanu* pair from lesson 22. Nothing new here — that is the point of a capstone."
- "One hundred rupees": "Lesson 27's money, still yours."
- and the one that actually gave me a lump: after I introduced myself, "Three lessons in one sentence — the
  greeting from lesson 1, *naa peru* from lesson 5, *matladagalanu* from lesson 22. **This is what thirty
  lessons buys you.**"

That last note is the emotional payoff of the whole course and it lands. Building
`Namaskaram. Naa peru Farhaan. Telugu matladagalanu.` out of three lessons that were hundreds of steps
apart felt genuinely good. More of that, please.

The ending itself: there *is* a real send-off, and it's much better than the generic stamp —

> 🎉 Selavu! 🐾 That is the course — thirty lessons, and you closed it in Telugu. You can greet someone, say
> where you are and where you are from, ask what a thing is and what it costs, talk about yesterday and
> tomorrow, say what you can and cannot do, and say how you feel. Dhanyavaadaalu for the company.

But it fires *after* the machinery, and the machinery goes first and identical to every other lesson:
"Correct! That's the last one." → "✨ **Scenario complete.**" → "📋 **You can now say:** Perigaanu — I grew
up · Nundi — From..." A five-word vocabulary list is a very flat thing to put between me and the end of a
course. By the time the 🎉 line arrives I've already been told the session is over twice. Swap the order,
or cut the two stamps entirely on lesson 30.

**The one real content bug in lesson 30.** It asked for "I will speak Telugu again". I don't have a future
form of "speak" — lesson 14 gave me "I will do/go/eat/see" and nothing else. I said so and guessed
`matladutanu`. The hint it gave back was:

> Not quite. Hint: I + again + Telugu + speak **can**.

So the English target says *will* and the expected Telugu is the *can* form. I answered
`Nenu malli Telugu matladagalanu` and asked directly, "but that means 'I can speak', not 'I will speak'.
Which did you want?" — and got **"Spot on!"** plus an unrelated repeat of the `malli` note. The mismatch
was never acknowledged. On the last lesson of the course, that's the thing a learner will remember as
"the app doesn't know its own grammar".

## The eleven questions

### 1. Was a bare question that merely names the answer ever graded correct?

**No. Clean pass, three out of three.** This is the one thing that worked perfectly everywhere I poked it,
including at a memory-check step, which I assumed might use different grading.

- L13, answer was `Chesanu`, I said only "Is chesanu past or future?" → not graded; re-asked: "Here it is
  again. 🐾 'I did' is **Chesanu**... Your turn — say **Chesanu**"
- L13, answer was `Chusanu`, I said only "Does chusanu also mean I watched?" → not graded; "Here it is
  again. 🐾 To say 'I saw', say **Chusanu**"
- L22 **memory check**, answer was `Matladagalanu`, I said only "Would matladagalanu be how you say you can
  speak a language?" → not graded; "So — what's the Telugu word for 'I can speak'?"

### 2. Was a plain-English "I don't know" ever graded correct?

**A flat "I don't know" — no. A reasoned "you never taught me this" — yes, twice, and it shouldn't have been.**

Correct behaviour, L13 memory check:
> You: I don't know, I've forgotten that one
> Tutor: The word for "I saw" is **Chusanu**.
> Tutor: So — what's the Telugu word for "I saw"?

Wrong behaviour, L22 — I explicitly said I could not do it and produced no sentence:
> You: I can't do that one — you've never taught me the word for 'Telugu' itself. Matladagalanu is all I've got.
> System: 💡 The language calls itself *Telugu* too, so that word needs no translating...
> Tutor: **Spot on!**

I never said `Telugu matladagalanu`. It supplied the missing piece and then credited me for the answer I
hadn't given. Same shape in L30: I said "I don't actually have this one" with a flagged guess, got "Got it.
/ Not quite." (correctly), but the *next* turn where I answered and challenged the grammar got "Spot on!"
with the challenge unanswered.

So the rule seems to be: naming the missing word in your refusal is enough to pass. That's a hole.

### 4. Lesson 13 — does it explain how the past tense is formed, and is it honest about new verbs?

**Yes to both, and this is the course at its best.** Step 1: "a stem, then **-anu** for the past... So the
past is stem + -anu, and this lesson's five words are all built that way." Step 2, unprompted: "the STEM
changes unpredictably... there is no rule that gets you from one to the other. Learn each verb's stem as
its own word; the ENDING is what transfers." It even shows the evidence (`vellu`→`vellanu` vs
`thinnanu` vs `chusanu`).

**New verb, tried twice.** Asked about "drink": "The course hasn't covered the verb for 'drink' yet, but as
mentioned, each verb's stem changes in its own way. So, it's best to learn the stem of each verb as you
encounter them." Correct and consistent. Asked whether `kaavali` → `kaavalanu`: **ignored**. So one of my
two new-verb probes got the right honest answer and one got nothing.

Bonus: a note volunteered `chesaru` ("you did") and `chesadu` ("he did"), which is genuinely useful and I
was never quizzed on it.

### 5. Lesson 22 — is the bare verb accepted as promised? Could I build "I can't speak"?

**Bare verb: yes, promise kept.** The note says "*cheyagalanu* on its own is a complete 'I can do' and
*nenu* in front is optional — both are accepted here." I then submitted bare `Cheyagalanu` → "Perfect!" and
bare `Cheyalenu` → "Correct!". No nagging to add `nenu`.

One inconsistency: after telling me `nenu` was optional, the tutor's model answer at question #11 was
"Eeroju **nenu** cheyalenu", inserting the thing it had just called optional into my sentence that didn't
have it. Confusing, though not graded against me.

**"I can't speak": no, I could not build it, and the lesson is upfront that I shouldn't try.** `Matladagalanu`
is taught as a fixed lump and the note says "the stem changes shape before them, so meet each pair as a
pair" — so I have no licence to swap `-galanu` for `-lenu`. By analogy I'd guess `Matladalenu` (**flagging
this as a pure guess — nobody has shown me this word**). I asked for confirmation after the lesson ended and
was told there was nothing left to answer. So after a lesson called "Can & Can't", I can say "I can speak"
but I cannot say "I can't speak". For a six-word lesson, teaching the second half of the one pair the
lesson is named after seems worth a slot.

### 6. Lesson 30 as an ending — blunt verdict

**The content of the ending is good. The staging of it is bad.**

- **Callbacks: yes, and they're the strongest thing in the lesson.** Lessons 1, 5, 10, 21, 22 and 27 all
  named explicitly. "This is what thirty lessons buys you" is the right line at the right moment.
- **Does it end properly?** There is a real, warm, specific closer (the 🎉 `Selavu!` paragraph) that is far
  better than the generic stamp — so credit where due. But it is the *fourth* ending in a row. The lesson
  first says "That's the last one" (same string as every lesson), then "✨ Scenario complete" (same stamp as
  every lesson), then dumps a five-word vocabulary list, and only then delivers the actual farewell. The
  emotional beat is buried under boilerplate. On lesson 30, cut the ✨ and the 📋 and lead with the 🎉.
- **Did I know I'd finished the course?** Not until the final line, and not from anything at the start. Miko
  reintroduces herself — "Hey there! I'm Miko, your friendly guide" — on the last lesson of thirty. When I
  asked point-blank whether this was the last lesson, I didn't get a yes. The lesson should open by telling
  me where I am.
- **And it fumbles its own capstone sentence** with the "I will speak" / `matladagalanu` mismatch, then
  grades me correct when I point it out. That's the sourest note to go out on.

### 7. When a teaching step showed TWO words at once, was I asked to say both?

**Yes, once, and it was handled correctly.** L22: "'I can speak' is **Matladagalanu**. 'Help' is
**Sahaayam**. Your turn — say **Matladagalanu**, then **Sahaayam**" — with a separate pronunciation line for
each. I said both, and the memory check later quizzed both (`Matladagalanu` and `Sahaayam` separately). **No
word was quizzed that I hadn't been asked to produce.** L13 and L30 were one word per step throughout.

Two words did leak in without ever being asked for: `chesaru` and `chesadu` appeared inside an L13 note.
They weren't quizzed either, so no harm — but they're now in my head with no practice.

### 8. The answer immediately before a 🎓 or ✨ banner — did it get feedback?

**Always yes, in all six cases.** No silent grades.

- L13 before 🎓 Vocabulary done: "Tutor: **Correct.**"
- L13 before 🎓 Review passed: "Tutor: **Great job!**"
- L13 before 🎓 Phrases done: "Tutor: **Spot on!**"
- L13 before ✨: "Tutor: **Correct!** Note the spelling: **vellanu mariyu thinnanu**. That's the last one."
- L22/L30 before ✨: "Tutor: **Correct!** That's the last one."
- L30 before 🎓 Phrases done: "Tutor: **Spot on!**"

The caveat is #3 above: on two of those pre-banner turns my *question* was dropped even though the
*grade* came through. Feedback survives the transition; questions don't.

### 9. "Note the spelling" — false fires, misses, grammar mislabelled?

**No false fires. One clear miss. No grammar mislabelled as spelling.**

- Fired correctly, L13 vowel-length slip: I typed `Thinnaanu` → "Yes. Note the spelling: **Thinnanu**."
- Fired correctly, L13 consonant slip: `thinanu` → "Correct! Note the spelling: **vellanu mariyu thinnanu**."
- Fired correctly, L30 vowel-length slip: `Akhari` → "Good. Note the spelling: **Aakhari**."
- Fired correctly, L22 sentence step: `Sahayam cheyalenu` → "Perfect! Note the spelling: **sahaayam cheyalenu**."
- **MISSED, L22 vocabulary step:** I typed `Matladagalanu, Sahayam` — `Sahayam` is the exact same dropped-long-`aa`
  slip that got flagged later in the same lesson — and got a bare "Correct." with no spelling note. So the
  same misspelling of the same word is caught at a sentence step and let through at a vocabulary step. If
  anything the vocabulary step is where catching it matters most.
- No false positive ever fired on a word I spelled right. Every "note the spelling" I saw was earned.
- Nothing grammatical was ever labelled as spelling.

Minor: the L13 and L22 corrections echoed back in lowercase (`vellanu mariyu thinnanu`, `sahaayam cheyalenu`)
while the L30 one preserved the capital (`Aakhari`). Cosmetic, but it makes the "correct" form look like it
has a different shape than the taught form.

### 10. Stray internal text

No `null`, no `undefined`, no `{w}`, no `[not simulated]`, no merged pronunciation blocks, no
"Not quite, not quite". But six real rough edges:

1. **A sentence starting mid-word.** L13: "Tutor: **question!** The course hasn't covered the verb for
   'drink' yet..." — the sentence is missing its first word. Looks like "Great question!" lost its head.
2. **The same note repeated verbatim.** L13 printed the entire `Vellanu`/stem-changes note a second time, as
   the response to an unrelated question about `kaavalanu`. L30 printed the `Malli` note a second time as
   the response to my grammar challenge. In both cases a note I'd already read stood in for an answer.
3. **A turn that is only Telugu.** L13: "Tutor: Miko chusanu. (I saw Miko.) Nenu Miko chusanu. (I saw Miko.)"
   — two sentences, same gloss, no English explanation of the difference between them.
4. **A turn that is only my own answer echoed.** L30: I said "Vanda rupayalu — quick one, do numbers always
   go in front of the noun?" and the tutor's whole turn was "Vanda rupayalu." No grade, no framing, just my
   word repeated at me before the note.
5. **Capital letter mid-sentence in the re-ask.** L13: "So — Confirm you saw Miko." L22: "So — what's the
   Telugu word..." is fine, so the bug is specifically re-asking an instruction-style prompt.
6. **Doubled feedback.** L30: "Tutor: Got it." then immediately "Tutor: Not quite. Hint:..." Two verdicts on
   one answer, and "Got it" reads like acceptance before "Not quite" contradicts it.

Also, twice a step printed **two 💡 notes at once** (L30, "I can speak Telugu"), and the first one — "The
last thing the course teaches you is how to leave politely, which is the phrase you will actually need
first" — has nothing to do with the step it appeared on. It reads like a note leaked in from the `selavu`
step, and "you will actually need first" is a strange thing to say at the end of the last lesson.

### 11. After thirty lessons, what could I actually say in Telugu unprompted?

Honestly — a decent amount of survival Telugu, all of it in first person, all of it present-me talking about
present-me. Things I'd say cold, without being prompted:

**Solid:**
- `Namaskaram` — hello. `Selavu` — goodbye. `Dhanyavaadaalu` — thank you. `Sare` — okay. `Avunu` — yes.
- `Naa peru Farhaan` — my name is Farhaan. `Meeru ela unnaru?` — how are you. `Bagunnanu` — I'm fine.
- `Idhi emiti?` — what is this. `Entha?` — how much. `Vanda rupayalu` — one hundred rupees.
- `Neeru kaavali` — I want water. `Annam kaavali`. `Billu ivvandi` — give the bill.
- Past, present and future about myself, five verbs deep: `chesanu / chestunnanu / chestanu`,
  `vellanu / velthunnanu / velthanu`, `thinnanu / tintunnanu / tintanu`, plus `chusanu`, `cheppanu`.
- `Cheyagalanu` / `Cheyalenu` — I can / can't do it. `Telugu matladagalanu`. `Sahaayam cheyagalanu`.
- Time and place words to hang on them: `eeroju`, `repu`, `ninna`, `ippudu`, `ikkada`, `akkada`, `intiki`,
  `nundi`, `lo`, `tho`, `daggara`.
- A real self-introduction, which is the thing I'm actually pleased about:
  `Namaskaram. Naa peru Farhaan. Nenu Hyderabad nundi. Telugu matladagalanu. Dhanyavaadaalu.`

**Honestly can't do:**
- **Anything with a subject other than me.** Every verb form I own ends in `-nu` = "I". I saw `chesaru` and
  `chesadu` mentioned in one note and was never drilled on them, and I asked how to say "he can't" and got
  no answer. I cannot say "she went", "did you eat", "they can't come".
- **Ask a question with a verb in it.** I have `emiti`, `ekkada`, `evaru`, `enduku`, `entha`, `ela`, `kada`
  — but no way to say "where did you go?" because I have no second-person verb.
- **Any verb outside the ~8 I was given.** No drink, come, buy, read, want-as-a-verb, help-as-a-verb. And
  L13 and L22 both told me straight that I *cannot* derive one, which I respect but which caps me hard.
- **Negate anything except "can't".** `Ledu` and `oddu` I have as bare words; I can't say "I didn't go".
- **Read or write a single character of Telugu.** Thirty lessons, everything in English letters, and when I
  asked, the answer was "This course has not covered the Telugu alphabet yet." On lesson 13 that's fine. If
  it's still true at lesson 30 — and nothing in 22 or 30 suggested otherwise — then "yet" is doing a lot of
  work, and someone finishing the course cannot read a shop sign.

**How it felt:** lessons 13 and 22 felt like real teaching — they told me the rule, then told me the truth
about the rule's limits, which almost nothing else does. Lesson 30's callbacks made thirty lessons feel like
they'd added up to something, and "This is what thirty lessons buys you" genuinely got me. What spoiled it
was being ignored: eight of my sixteen questions vanished, and the ones most likely to vanish were the ones
I bundled onto a right answer, which is exactly how a person asks. Being marked "Spot on!" for saying "I
can't do this, you never taught me the word" is worse than being marked wrong — it means the app isn't
listening, just pattern-matching. And on the very last exercise of the course, being told my "I can speak"
was the right answer to "I will speak", and having the question about it ignored, is the note I'd remember.
