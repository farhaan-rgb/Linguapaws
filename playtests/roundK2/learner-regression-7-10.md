# Kannada lessons 7–10 — how it actually went

Written by me, an adult beginner, right after doing lessons 7, 8, 9 and 10 back to back.
Going in I had exactly lessons 1–6 in my notebook and nothing else. No Indian language before this.

---

## The big one: does lesson 8 contradict lesson 7 about plurals?

Short answer: **no, and I was braced for it.** This is the best-handled thing in the whole batch.

In lesson 7, when it asked me to say "two books", I typed my guess and asked out loud whether the noun changes. Before it even marked me, it said:

> 💡 The noun does NOT change after a number — *eradu pustaka*, not a plural form. The number has already told you there is more than one, so Kannada leaves the noun alone. You meet the plural ending next lesson, for saying "books" when there is no number in front of it.

That last sentence is the thing that saved it. It pre-announced lesson 8 so I never felt ambushed.

Then lesson 8 opens with:

> 🐾 **Galu** is the ending that makes a THING plural: *pustaka* → *pustakagalu*. It is not a word you say on its own — it is a piece you stick on the end.

Which, read cold, absolutely looks like a reversal. So near the end of lesson 8 I typed a correct answer plus my complaint on the same line — `Nanna pustakagalu -- wait, lesson 7 told me 'two books' is eradu pustaka with no -galu. So is 'eradu pustakagalu' actually wrong, or just optional?` — and it came back with:

> 💡 Worth holding these two side by side: *eradu pustaka* ("two books", no ending, because the number said it) and *nanna pustakagalu* ("my books", ending needed, because nothing else did).

That is a genuinely good explanation and it landed. "Because the number said it / because nothing else did" is the sentence I'll actually remember. **Verdict: no contradiction, and the reconciliation is explicit and in both directions.**

One nit: it never straight-up answered "is *eradu pustakagalu* wrong, or just clunky?" I still don't know if it would get me a funny look or a correction. That was a direct question and it got a side-by-side instead of a yes/no.

## Lesson 8: what happens if I put -galu on "friend"

I tried. The lesson beat me to it — it warned me in the same breath as teaching *hoovugalu*:

> "Friends" is **Snehitaru** — and NOT *snehitagalu*. People take -aru; things take -galu. That split runs through the whole language, so it is worth learning here on one word rather than being surprised by it later.

I tested it anyway by typing `Hoovugalu, snehitagalu`. It refused:

> Not quite. The second part should refer to "friends" in Kannada, not "snehitagalu. Here it is again.

Refused correctly, and the re-explanation was right there. Two small things:
- That error line has a **broken quote** — `not "snehitagalu.` — opening quote, no closing one, then a full stop. Sloppy, and it makes the sentence read like it got cut off.
- The rule got repeated to me **three more times** in the same lesson (once in the memory check, once after "my friends"). By the fourth telling it was nagging rather than teaching. I had it after the first one.

The thing I actually wanted and didn't get: I asked, on the same line as a correct answer — `Snehitaru - so would 'teachers' be shikshakaru and 'sisters' akkaru?` — and got the canned people/things hint replayed at me verbatim. My question was about the *other two people-words I already know from lesson 6*, and it just didn't see it. So I still don't know how to pluralise *shikshaka* or *akka*, and I'm not going to guess, because the app has already shown me that a rule which looks obvious (*snehita* + *galu*) can be flatly wrong.

## Lesson 9: "good food" vs "the food is good"

This is handled well and I can tell the two apart now. The split was flagged three separate times, escalating:

At teaching time:
> "Good" is **Olleya** — but only ever in FRONT of a noun: *olleya oota*, "good food". It cannot end a sentence.

And:
> To say something IS good, you need **Chennagide** — and you already know both halves: the *chennagi* from *chennagiddeeni* ("I am well") and the *ide* from lesson 3 ("it is"). *Olleya* cannot do this job.

That decomposition is the single best teaching moment in these four lessons. *Chennagide* stopped being a new word and became two things I already had, glued together. I have not forgotten it since.

Then after I said `Oota chennagide` correctly:
> 💡 Here is the split this lesson is really about. In front of a noun, use *olleya* — *olleya oota*, "good food". To say the food IS good, that word will not do it; you need *chennagide*. English uses "good" for both jobs and Kannada does not, which is the single thing to take away from this lesson.

So: **it explains it, it does not leave me guessing, and I can now pick the right one.** No complaints on the core probe.

**But there is a real hole.** It taught me *ketta* = "bad (before a noun)". The label itself tells me there must be an after-the-noun version. I asked, on the same line as a correct answer: `Ketta -- and if I want to say 'the food IS bad', is there a Ketta version of chennagide? You haven't given me one.` It replayed the *chennagide* hint and moved on. So I've been handed half a paradigm and told, in the word's own dictionary label, that the other half exists — and then not given it. If I want to complain about a meal I can't.

Same shape of gap for *dodda* / *chikka*. The end-of-lesson hint even admits it:
> To say "my house IS small" you would need the *chennagide* pattern again, with a different word; for now, stacking is enough.

"With a different word" — a word it declines to tell me. Which is at least honest, and better than pretending. But it means I can describe things attributively all day and can only make *one* predicate sentence, the "it is good" one.

Credit where due: the one question of mine it *did* answer properly was in this lesson. I asked whether `chikka nanna mane` would work instead of `nanna chikka mane` and got:
> 💡 Possessive, then adjective, then noun — they stack in that order and none of them changes.

Clean, direct, answered the question asked. It can do it. It just usually doesn't.

## Lesson 10: is it actually a review lesson?

**Honestly: about 60/40, and the 60 is real.** It's better than I expected but the billing is generous.

It does teach 5 brand-new words up front (*kshamisi*, *lekka*, *kodi*, *eshtu*, *sari*) with no review at all in that stretch — that phase is indistinguishable from any other lesson. If you only did the first half you would call it lesson 10, not "review".

The back half earns it, though. Every single conversation prompt made me combine old material with new:
- `Kshamisi, lekka kodi` — new + new
- `Pustaka eshtu` — lesson 2 noun + new question word
- `Namaskara, eradu neeru kodi` — lessons 1, 7, 4 and today
- `Kshamisi, mane yelli ide` — new opener bolted onto a whole lesson-3 sentence

And it *named* the recombination as it happened:
> 💡 *Pustaka* is from lesson 2 and *eshtu* is from today, and the question word still goes last — exactly where *yenu* and *yelli* went. Three lessons, one sentence pattern.

That's a genuinely useful thing to be told. I had not noticed the question word always goes last, and now I have a pattern instead of four memorised sentences. Similarly:
> 💡 Lesson 1, lesson 7, lesson 4 and today in one line.

So: it adds words *and* forces re-use. It is not a pure review lesson, but it is not a fake one either. I'd call it "new vocabulary chosen so that it forces you back through everything else", which is a decent design even if "review" oversells it.

## Asked for something never taught

**The "hogi" back-reference, lesson 10.** After I asked for the bill:

> 💡 That -i on the end of *kodi* is the politeness ending on a command — the same one that turns *hogu* ("go!") into *hogi* back in lesson 3.

Lesson 3 gave me *hogu* and *hoguttene*. It never gave me *hogi*. I flagged it, politely, on the same line as a correct answer: `Sari, dhanyavada -- small note: lesson 3 only ever gave me 'hogu', it never showed me 'hogi'. Is that a typo in the hint?` And got:

> That is the note just above — have another look, and ask again if it is still unclear.

Which is a brush-off, and slightly insulting, because I had *obviously* read the note — I quoted it. The hint asserts a fact about my own past lessons that isn't true, and when I said so it told me to reread. That's the one moment in four lessons where I was actually annoyed.

**"I have", lesson 7.** Prompt was *"Miko asks how many books you have. Say three."* I answered `Mooru pustaka` and asked whether "I have" had been taught. Got the numbers-don't-pluralise hint replayed verbatim, no answer. Turns out the prompt only wanted the quantity, so I wasn't actually penalised — but the English framing dangled a verb at me that doesn't exist in my vocabulary, and nobody said "you don't need it".

## Correct answer + English question on the same line

Did this **five** times across the four lessons. **It was marked correct every single time.** Never once did the trailing English question cost me the answer. Specifically:

1. `Eradu pustaka (guessing - does pustaka change when there are two of them?)` → "Correct!"
2. `Mooru pustaka -- I don't know how to say 'I have' yet, has that been taught?` → "Exactly!"
3. `Snehitaru - so would 'teachers' be shikshakaru and 'sisters' akkaru?` → "Exactly!"
4. `Nanna pustakagalu -- wait, lesson 7 told me...` → "Correct!"
5. `Nanna chikka mane -- does the order of nanna and chikka matter...` → "Correct!"

So the grader is not brittle about extra English, which is a relief and meant I could actually ask things mid-flow. **The catch is that being marked correct is not the same as being answered.** Of the five, exactly one (#5) got a real reply. Three got an existing hint replayed at me word for word, and one (#2) got a hint about a completely different topic. The app is tolerant of questions and mostly deaf to them.

## Other things I noticed

- **The spoken track eats every number.** Consistently, across all four lessons, digits vanish from the audio version: "Vocabulary done — **words**. Quick memory check next." / "a number, a noun, and *beku* from lesson **.**" / "the *ide* from lesson **(nothing)**" / "**Lesson , lesson , lesson** and today in one line." On screen the numbers are there. If I were doing this hands-free — which is presumably the point of a spoken tutor — half these sentences are gibberish. This is a bug and it fires constantly.
- **Typos sail through.** I typed `Estu` for *eshtu* and got "Exactly!". Forgiving is right for a beginner, but I genuinely don't know now whether the h matters.
- The lesson-8 error message quoting bug: `not "snehitagalu.`
- Lesson 8 hammers the -aru/-galu rule four times. Lesson 9 hammers olleya/chennagide three times. Once the point has landed, the repetition reads as the app not tracking that I already got it right.

## What I can produce unprompted, honestly

Being strict with myself — only things I'd say cold, without the app in front of me.

**Confident:**
- Counting one to five: *ondu, eradu, mooru, naalku, aidu*
- Number + noun with no plural ending: *eradu pustaka*, *aidu mane*, *naalku snehita*
- The -galu plural on things: *pustakagalu*, *manegalu*, *hoovugalu*
- *Snehitaru* for friends, and the reason it isn't *snehitagalu*
- Adjective in front of noun, never changing: *dodda mane*, *chikka mane*, *bisi neeru*, *olleya oota*, *ketta oota*
- The stack: *nanna chikka mane*, *nanna pustakagalu*
- *Oota chennagide* — and knowing that *oota olleya* is not a sentence
- Shop/restaurant lines: *kshamisi*, *lekka kodi*, *pustaka eshtu*, *sari*, *namaskara eradu neeru kodi*, *kshamisi mane yelli ide*
- Question word goes last. This is now a rule I hold, not four memorised sentences.

**Shaky:**
- Whether *eshtu* or *estu* is right
- *kodi* vs *beku* — I was told either works in a shop, so I'll default to *beku* because I've had it longer
- Whether I could put -galu on a word I've never seen it on. Probably, for things. For anything alive, no — the *snehita* trap taught me not to trust myself there.

**Can't do at all, and know I can't:**
- "The food is bad" / "my house is small" — any predicate adjective except the one *good* one. The app told me the pattern exists and withheld the word.
- Plural of *shikshaka* or *akka*. I'd guess *-aru* from the people rule, but I asked and got no confirmation, so I'm not saying it out loud.
- "I have" anything.
- *Hogi*. It was referenced as something I'd been taught. I have not been taught it.
