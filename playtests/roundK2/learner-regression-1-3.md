# Kannada lessons 1–3 — playtest notes

Adult beginner, native English, zero prior Indian-language exposure. Sessions `K2rA1`, `K2rA2`, `K2rA3`.
Writing this like I'd tell a friend who's about to try it.

## The headline: the wrong-word-from-a-pair probe

This is the thing I was most worried about, and honestly — **it passed, four times out of four.** I tried
to fool it with the near/far pairs and it caught every one, named the meaning error, and never once
called it a spelling slip.

**Probe 1 — vocab drill, lesson 2.** It asked me for *Adu* ("that"). I typed `Idu`:

> Not quite. You're using a word that means "this" instead of the word for "that. Here it is again. “That” is **Adu** — something further off. […]

**Probe 2 — sentence level, lesson 2.** Asked to say 'What is that?', I typed `Idu yenu?`:

> Not quite. You used the word for 'this' instead of the word for 'that'.

**Probe 3 — vocab drill, lesson 3.** Asked for `Alli`, then `Mane`. I typed `Illi Mane`:

> Not quite. You used the word for "here" instead of "there. Here it is again. “There” is **Alli** — the far one. […]

**Probe 4 — sentence level, lesson 3.** Asked to say 'The house is there', I typed `Mane illi ide`:

> Not quite. You used the word for 'here' instead of the word for 'there'.

So: no false accepts, no "check your spelling" fudging, and it caught the wrong word even when it was
buried mid-sentence with the rest of the sentence correct. That's exactly what I wanted.

Two small blemishes in that otherwise-good behaviour:

1. **The message has a broken quote mark, twice.** `instead of the word for "that.` and
   `instead of "there.` — the closing quote is missing and the period ends up inside nothing. It reads
   as a typo the *app* made, which is a bit undermining when it's correcting *my* typing.
2. **On probe 4, the wrong answer still triggered the teaching hint, which handed me the answer.**
   After telling me I'd said "here", it showed: *"without it `mane alli` is just 'house there'…"* — so
   the correct word was printed right there before I retried. Free answer for getting it wrong.

## Typos vs. wrong words — treated completely differently, and correctly

Three deliberate typos, all accepted-with-a-note:

- L1, dropped a doubled n: `Chenagiddeeni` → **"Good. Note the spelling: Chennagiddeeni."**
- L2, dropped a vowel: `Pustka` → **"Exactly! Note the spelling: Pustaka."**
- L3, dropped a doubled l — the exact case I was told to try: `Yeli` for *Yelli* → **"Correct. Note the spelling: Yelli."**

So the distinction is clean and legible: **a misspelling of the right word passes with a correction; the
right spelling of the wrong word fails with a meaning explanation.** That's the correct split and it never
blurred. Nice.

## The optional-pronoun probe (lesson 1's last exercise)

**It accepted me, and confirmed the rule unprompted.** Final exercise was "say hello, say you are fine,
and ask how they are". I dropped both *naanu* and *neevu*: `Namaskara, chennagiddeeni, hege iddeera?`

> 💡 That is the whole exchange in one breath. Both pronouns are optional, exactly as the notes said — *Namaskara, chennagiddeeni, hegiddeera?* is just as correct, and is what you will hear most often.
> Correct! That's the last one.

It also held up earlier in the lesson — `Hege iddeera?` with no *neevu* was accepted, and it volunteered
that *hege iddeera* split into two words and *hegiddeera* fused are "equally correct and equally accepted".
So the lesson tells the truth and the checker honours it. No complaints at all here.

One nice moment: when it asked for "I am fine, and you?" I answered and flagged that **no word for "and"
had ever been taught**. It handled that properly rather than pretending:

> No word for “and” is needed here — Kannada just sets the two halves side by side. *Matte* (“and”) exists, but a bare *neevu?* is how this is really said.

That's the right way to answer a learner poking at a gap.

## Writing English on the same line as my answer

Did this four times. **It never once cost me a correct answer** — every time my Kannada was right, I got
marked right regardless of the English trailing after it. Good.

- L1: `Iddeera — is this only used with Neevu, or can I use it with a friend too?` → **"Exactly!"**
- L2: `Idu yenu? — guessing at the order here, does the question word go last?` → it *actually answered me*: **"You got it right! In Kannada, the pointing word comes first, and the question word 'yenu' goes last."** That was genuinely great.
- L3: `Mane yelli? — not sure if that's right, is yelli always at the end, and should ide be in there too?` → **"Perfect!"**, with a hint confirming the yelli-goes-last rule.
- L3 final: `Alli hoguttene — though by the -ge rule shouldn't it be allige since I'm going TO there? Guessing.` → **"Correct! That's the last one."**

**But the question-answering is a lottery.** Only one of my four questions actually got answered. The
other three fired a hint that was picked by *some* mechanism unrelated to what I'd asked:

- I asked whether *iddeera* works with a friend. It re-showed the definition of *iddeera* I'd already been given. Didn't address "friend" at all.
- I asked whether *Namaskara* is too formal for a friend. It showed me the **hegiddeera fusion hint** — completely unrelated to my question.
- I asked whether it should be *allige* by the -ge rule. It replayed the *mane* → *manege* hint, which is adjacent but never says whether *allige* is a real form. So I still don't know.
- I asked whether *ide* belonged in "Mane yelli?". Never answered.

It's not that answering is broken — L2 proved it can do it well. It's that it looks like the hint is
chosen by keyword/step rather than by what I asked, so most of the time I get a canned card that happens
to mention a word I typed. As a learner that's mildly maddening: it *looks* like an answer, so you read
it carefully, and then realise it didn't address you.

## The genuinely unfair one

Lesson 2, conversation stage: **"Someone points to a book. Ask them what it is."**

I answered `Adu yenu?` — someone *else* is pointing at the book, so from where I'm standing it's over
there. Rejected. And here's the annoying part: the first time I asked it with an English aside attached
(`Adu yenu? — though I honestly can't tell from the prompt whether the book is near me or far…`) I got
**no "not quite" at all** — just a hint card re-explaining *Adu* and then:

> So — Someone points to a book. Ask them what it is.

I couldn't tell if I'd been marked wrong or if it had just ignored me. I retyped it bare to find out, and
*then* got the real verdict:

> Not quite. You used the word for 'that' instead of the word for 'this'.

Two problems stacked:

1. **The prompt gives me no way to know the intended distance.** "Someone points to a book" is
   distance-neutral in English. The whole lesson is built on near-vs-far being a real semantic
   distinction — so the prompt has to *specify* the distance or it's a coin flip. Compare the very next
   exercise, which does it right: "Point to a **distant** object and ask 'What is that?'". That one's fair.
   This one isn't, and I got dinged for the app's ambiguity, not my error.
2. **A wrong answer with English attached suppressed the error message.** The bare wrong answer told me
   plainly what was wrong; the same wrong answer with a comment on the end just silently re-asked. If
   you're going to accept mixed-language input (and you should — it worked great everywhere else), the
   *failure* path needs to survive it too. Right now writing out loud makes your errors less legible.

## Other things worth fixing

**The text-to-speech eats numbers.** Every single time, and sometimes it wrecks the sentence:

- On screen: `**Vocabulary done** — 6 words.` / Spoken: `Vocabulary done — words.` (happened in all three lessons: 6, 5 and 7 words)
- On screen: `…the word for “your”, *nimma*, which is lesson 5 — *Nimma hesaru yenu?*` / Spoken: `…which is lesson — Nimma hesaru yenu?`
- On screen: `Note the contrast with lesson 2 — …` / Spoken: `Note the contrast with lesson — …`

If I were listening rather than reading, the last two are just broken sentences. Whatever strips markdown
for the spoken track is clearly eating digits.

**Lesson 3's third vocab step is overloaded.** One turn threw at me: *Alli* ("there"), the near/far vowel
contrast, *Mane* ("house"), **and** the entire `-ge` destination case ending — then asked me to repeat two
words. Every other step introduces one thing. Same again two steps later: *Hogu*, the `-i` politeness
ending, *and* `Hoguttene` with its `-ttene` person marker, all in one breath. Both of those deserve to be
split.

**Asked for something never taught, lesson 3:** "Someone asks 'Neevu yelli?'. Tell them you are here."
I'd been explicitly told minutes earlier that *ide* is "used of a thing rather than a person" — so I had
**no** way to say "I am" about myself. I said so:

> Illi — but you haven't taught me how to say 'I am' for a person; you said ide is only for things, so I don't know the word for 'I am here'

It marked me **"Perfect!"** on the bare `Illi`, then showed a hint revealing the expected form was
`naanu illi` — i.e. a bare pronoun plus a bare place word, **no verb at all**. Which quietly contradicts
what it had just drilled into me: that `mane alli` alone is "just 'house there'" and you *need* ide to
make it a statement. Apparently people don't need it and things do — but nobody told me that, I had to
reverse-engineer it from a hint that appeared *after* I'd answered. And my direct question about how to
say "I am" for a person still went unanswered.

Also, that step fired an irrelevant hint first — a card about **Alli / "there"** — when I had used *Illi*
correctly. Same hint-selection problem as above.

**Small stuff:**

- Lesson 2 ends without the little 🎉 payoff line that 1 and 3 both get. Feels abrupt, like it dropped a beat.
- Lesson 3's recap lists `Mane — House / home` but never `manege`, even though the `-ge` ending was taught, drilled and used in the final exercises. If I only kept the recap card I'd have lost it.
- Lesson 3 refers back to *chennagiddeeni* and "lesson 2" as if I'd carried them over, which I had — but each scenario is a separate session, so a learner who did 3 first would be told to recall something they'd never seen.

## What I can actually produce unprompted now

Being honest with myself — this is what I could type cold, without a hint on screen:

**Solid, would say it without thinking:**
- `Namaskara` — hello
- `Chennagiddeeni` — I am fine (and I know the `-eeni` ending is the "I")
- `Hege iddeera?` / `Hegiddeera?` — how are you (polite), and I know both forms are fine
- `Namaskara, chennagiddeeni, hege iddeera?` — the whole greeting exchange, pronouns dropped
- `Idu` / `Adu` — this / that, and I'm confident which is which
- `Idu yenu?` / `Adu yenu?` — what is this / what is that
- `Idu pustaka` — this is a book, and I know there's no word for "is" in it
- `Illi` / `Alli` — here / there
- `Mane yelli?` — where is the house
- `Mane alli ide` — the house is there
- `Alli hogu` — go there
- `Manege hoguttene` — I am going home

**Know the word, shaky on using it:**
- `Naanu` / `Neevu` — I know what they mean; I mostly know I can leave them out, but "when do I actually *want* one" is only half-clear (the one case I was given is `neevu?` for "and you?")
- `Hesaru` — name. I can say `Hesaru yenu?` but I can't say "your name" — *nimma* was mentioned and explicitly deferred to lesson 5
- `Hogu` vs `hogi` — I was told `-i` is the polite command ending but never drilled `hogi`, so I'd hesitate
- `Ide` — I can use it for a house. For anything else, and especially for a person, I'm guessing

**Told about but couldn't produce:**
- `Matte` ("and") — named in passing, never practised
- `Allige` — I *think* the `-ge` rule should apply to *alli* but I asked and never got an answer, so I'd avoid it
- "I am here" as a full sentence with a verb — still don't have it. `naanu illi` appeared in a hint after I'd already answered; I don't trust it enough to use it
- Any actual Kannada script. Everything so far has been romanised and I wouldn't recognise a single letter written down

**Overall:** the error handling on meaning-vs-spelling is genuinely good and the pronoun rule is honest.
The two things that would most improve the next round are (a) fix the "someone points to a book" prompt so
distance isn't a coin flip, and (b) make the hint that fires after a free-text question actually relate to
the question — right now half of them are non-sequiturs that look like answers.
