# Kannada, lessons 1–10 — full play-through by a total beginner

Adult native English speaker. Zero prior exposure to Kannada or any Indian language.
Everything below is from the ten sessions themselves; the exam in Section 2 was written
after closing the app, with nothing to look back at.

---

## Section 1: the play-through

### Lesson 1 — greetings
Gentle start, and honestly the best-taught lesson of the ten. Six words, then a quick
recall check, then sentences. The thing that made it click was this note after my first
full sentence:

> "*Chennagiddeeni* is a whole sentence on its own — the -eeni on the end IS the 'I', so
> there is no separate word for 'am', and *naanu* can be left out."

That is a genuinely useful idea to hand someone in their first five minutes, and it kept
paying off all the way to lesson 10.

Two snags. First, I mistyped *Chennagideeni* (one d) and got **"Good. Note the spelling:
Chennagiddeeni."** — marked correct with a spelling nudge, which is exactly right and
which I wish the app had done more of later.

Second, and this one actually annoyed me: it asked me to say **"I am fine, and you?"**
when it had never taught me a word for "and". I said so explicitly —

> me: "I can say chennagiddeeni but you haven't taught me the word for 'and' — how do I
> say 'and you?'"

— and it did **not** answer. It replayed the earlier tip about *chennagiddeeni* verbatim
and said **"Not quite. Hint: I-am-fine + you."** Only after I produced *Chennagiddeeni,
neevu?* did it tell me that no word for "and" is needed and that *matte* exists. That
explanation was the answer to my question and it was sitting right there — it just refused
to give it until I'd already guessed. That pattern (my question ignored, an old tip
replayed, the real answer withheld until after a correct guess) repeated in lesson 5.

Also, from lesson 1 onward: the on-screen banner says **"Vocabulary done — 6 words"** but
the spoken line reads **"Vocabulary done — words."** The number is stripped out of every
spoken line, in every lesson. Same bug eats lesson numbers later ("back in lesson ",
"Lesson , lesson , lesson and today in one line"). If I were listening rather than
reading, several tips would be nonsense.

### Lesson 2 — this/that, what
Very clean. *Idu* / *adu*, and the app made a real point of the i-/a- near/far vowel pair —
"get it wrong and you have said the opposite". That framing was the single most useful
thing in the whole course, because the same pair then showed up on *illi/alli* and
*ivaru/avaru* and I got both for free.

Two rules stated crisply and both stuck: pointing word first, question word **last**. And
the no-"is" rule: *idu pustaka* = "this is a book", with the explicit warning that *ide*
would be wrong here.

Small thing: the last drill asked **"Ask 'What is the name?'"** and I gave *Hesaru yenu?*.
It was accepted, but the note told me the real sentence needs *nimma*, which is lesson 5.
So the exercise is a slightly unnatural half-sentence that exists only because the app
hasn't taught the possessive yet. Fine, but it felt like filler.

### Lesson 3 — where / going
Big lesson, seven words, and the first one where I had to guess a whole sentence shape.
*Mane yelli ide?* — I reasoned it from lesson 2 (question word last) plus the new *ide*
closing the sentence, and got it. The tip then said:

> "*Yelli* went to the end."

It didn't. *Ide* is at the end; *yelli* is second of three. The rule as stated is wrong in
the very sentence it's explaining. What's actually true is "the question word goes late,
before the verb" — and because I'd internalised the literal version, I was briefly unsure
later whether *Pustaka eshtu?* needed something after it.

The *-ge* ending on *manege* was taught well and stated as a rule ("that ending goes on the
noun, and it is how Kannada marks any destination"), which is why it survived to the exam.

One place I flagged a real gap. The app said *ide* is "used of a thing rather than a
person", then immediately asked me to say I'm here. I answered:

> me: "Naanu illi — though I'm not sure, you said ide is only for things, and you haven't
> taught me the 'am' for a person"

It said **"Perfect!"** but never addressed the question. I still do not know how to say "he
is here" or "my sister is here" with a verb — I only know how to say it with the verb
missing. That gap is still open at the end of lesson 10 (see Part 2 #8 in the exam).

### Lesson 4 — want / don't want
Good lesson, and *beku* / *beda* is a lovely pair to hand a beginner.

But: I answered **"Naanu neeru beku"** and got **"Perfect!"** — and *then* was told

> "Two words and no 'I'. *Beku* means 'is wanted' … If you ever need to be explicit it is
> *nanage neeru beku*, literally 'to me water is wanted'."

So my sentence used the wrong pronoun form (*naanu*, not *nanage*) by the app's own
explanation, and was marked perfect anyway. That's the opposite of the lesson-1 spelling
nudge, and it's the kind of thing that quietly cements an error. I only avoided it because
the note happened to appear.

*Illa* was taught with a nice warning against *illi* — genuinely helpful, those two are one
letter apart and I'd have mixed them up.

### Lesson 5 — possessives
Easy content, one properly bad exercise.

> Tutor: "Point at a book across the room and tell Miko it is your book."

I said *Adu nanna pustaka* — "that is my book" — because in English, when you tell someone
about a book that is yours, it is **your** book from your own mouth. Marked wrong:
**"You used the word for 'my' instead of 'your."** (Note the missing closing quote mark in
the app's own error text.) I pushed back:

> me: "Hang on — 'tell Miko it is your book' reads to me as MY book. Do you mean it's
> Miko's book?"

Again: no answer, just a replayed tip about possessive word order, then **"So — Point at a
book across the room and tell Miko it is your book."** I complied with *Adu nimma pustaka*
and it passed.

Then in **lesson 6** the app asked **"Tell Miko this person is your teacher"** — same
grammatical frame — and the answer it wanted was ***nanna* shikshaka**, "MY teacher". So
"your X" means *nimma* in lesson 5 and *nanna* in lesson 6. That is a straight
self-contradiction across two lessons, on the exact point I'd already been marked wrong
for. I only got lesson 6 right by ignoring what lesson 5 taught me.

Also *aatana* ("his") was drilled in vocabulary and review and then **never used in a
sentence I had to build** except one — I have it as a flashcard, not as language.

### Lesson 6 — who / people
Fine. *Ivaru* covering "this person / he / she (polite)" was well explained, including the
useful note that English's "she" has no separate word here.

*Avaru* ("that person") was mentioned once, in passing, inside the *Ivaru* teaching line —
never drilled, never in the review, never in the summary card. I remembered it only because
I'd been primed to hunt for i-/a- pairs. That's luck, not teaching.

Notably, *Aatana* from lesson 5 and *Avara* never reappeared here, where they obviously
belong.

### Lesson 7 — numbers 1–5
Five numbers, no complications, and one genuinely excellent rule:

> "The noun does NOT change after a number — *eradu pustaka*, not a plural form."

Delivered right before lesson 8 teaches the plural, and then explicitly contrasted again in
lesson 8 (*eradu pustaka* vs *nanna pustakagalu*). That's the best-sequenced pair of ideas
in the course and it is the one grammar point I am completely solid on.

The final drill, "ask for two waters", combined three lessons and felt great — first time
the course felt like it was building something.

### Lesson 8 — plurals
*-galu* for things, *-aru* for people, with the memory hook "ask whether it is a person".
Clear, drilled, contrasted, summarised. This is how all of it should be taught.

Nothing confused me here. Only quibble: it teaches *snehitaru* but never *shikshakaru* or
*akkandiru* or any second people-plural, so I know the rule from exactly one example and
can't be sure I'd apply it right to a new word.

### Lesson 9 — adjectives
The *olleya* / *chennagide* split is a real and important distinction and the app made it
the explicit point of the lesson. Good.

*Ketta* ("bad") — taught, reviewed, and then **never used in a single sentence**. Same
dead-flashcard problem as *aatana*.

And the closing note said, of "my house IS small":

> "you would need the *chennagide* pattern again, with a different word"

…without ever telling me the word. So I can say "the food is good" and I cannot say "the
house is small", and I've been told there's a pattern I don't have. Frustrating way to end
a lesson.

### Lesson 10 — shop/restaurant
Nice consolidation. *Kodi* vs *beku* explained as complementary rather than competing,
which felt like real language. And the callback tying *kodi*'s -i to *hogi*'s -i from lesson
3 was the one moment the course felt like a system rather than a list — except it was
spoken as **"back in lesson "**, number stripped, again.

*Pustaka eshtu?* I guessed from the question-word-last rule and it worked. The final line,
*Kshamisi, mane yelli ide?*, I built with no hesitation at all, which after ten lessons is
a fair result.

### Cross-cutting things
- **The number-stripping in spoken text is in every single lesson.** Audio-first learners
  lose every word count and every cross-reference.
- **Direct questions from me were never answered — three times.** Each time the app
  replayed a stale tip and re-asked. Twice the answer existed and was shown *after* I'd
  guessed correctly.
- **Grading is inconsistent.** A typo got a gentle correction (lesson 1); a genuinely wrong
  pronoun form got "Perfect!" (lesson 4); a defensible reading of an ambiguous English
  prompt got marked wrong (lesson 5).
- **Vocabulary taught but never used in a sentence**: *aatana*, *avara*, *ketta*, *avaru*,
  *chikka* (used once), *hesaru* only ever in one frame. Roughly a fifth of the words are
  flashcards, not language.
- **Never taught, and I noticed the hole each time**: "and" (*matte*, named but not
  taught), the "is/are" verb for a person (told *ide* doesn't do it, never given the
  alternative), "small/big" as a predicate, "you" informal, any verb except *hogu*.

## Section 2: the exam

Written from memory with the app closed. Nothing looked up.

### Part 1 — English → Kannada

1. **Hello, I am fine.** — *Namaskara, naanu chennagiddeeni.* (I'm fairly sure *naanu* is
   optional: *Namaskara, chennagiddeeni.* is what I was told people actually say.)
2. **How are you? (polite)** — *Neevu hege iddeera?* — or fused, *Hegiddeera?* Both were
   said to be equally correct.
3. **What is this?** — *Idu yenu?*
4. **Where is the house?** — *Mane yelli ide?*
5. **I want water.** — *Neeru beku.* (No pronoun. If forced to be explicit it's *nanage
   neeru beku* — I was told that once but never practised it, so I'd say the two-word one.)
6. **What is your name?** — *Nimma hesaru yenu?*
7. **Who is this?** — *Ivaru yaaru?*
8. **Two books** — *Eradu pustaka.* (Deliberately NOT *pustakagalu* — the number already
   marks plural.)
9. **My friends** — *Nanna snehitaru.* (Not *snehitagalu* — people take -aru.)
10. **The food is good.** — *Oota chennagide.* (Not *olleya* — that only goes before a
    noun.)
11. **Ask for the bill.** — *Lekka kodi.*
12. **Where is my book?** — *Nanna pustaka yelli ide?* — reasoning: I know *nanna pustaka*
    and I know *mane yelli ide?*, and I was told possessives just slot in front without
    anything else moving. Confident but it is assembled, not memorised.
13. **This is my house.** — *Idu nanna mane.* (No "is" — *idu pustaka* pattern.)
14. **I don't want hot water.** — *Bisi neeru beda.*
15. **Who is that person (further away)?** — *Avaru yaaru?* — reasoning: *ivaru* is "this
    person", and the app mentioned once in passing that *avaru* is "that person", which
    fits the *idu/adu*, *illi/alli* i-near/a-far pattern. I never drilled *avaru*, so this
    is pattern-matching, not recall. Medium confidence.
16. **Three flowers** — *Mooru hoovu.* Singular deliberately, by the number rule. I want to
    flag that I am confident in the *rule* and only inferring the *application* — I was
    only ever shown the rule on *pustaka* and *mane*, never on *hoovu*.
17. **Excuse me, where is the food?** — *Kshamisi, oota yelli ide?*

### Part 2 — Kannada → English

1. **Nimma hesaru yenu?** — "What is your name?"
2. **Mane alli ide.** — "The house is there." (*alli* = there, the far one.)
3. **Naanu manege hoguttene.** — "I am going home / I am going to the house." The *-ge* is
   "to", the *-ttene* is "I".
4. **Oota beda.** — "I don't want food." / "No food, thanks." It's a refusal, not the word
   "no".
5. **Ivaru nanna shikshaka.** — "This is my teacher." / "He (or she) is my teacher" —
   *ivaru* covers both and is the polite form for someone present.
6. **Eradu pustaka beku.** — "I want two books."
7. **Adu chennagide.** — "That is good."
8. **Nanna akka illi.** — "My elder sister is here." I'll flag this one: there's no verb in
   it, and I was told *ide* is for things not people, but I was **never taught what the
   person version is**. So I can read this sentence but I could not have written it with
   confidence, and I don't know whether it's complete Kannada or clipped.

### Part 3 — explain the rule

1. **`illi` vs `alli`** — *illi* = here (near me), *alli* = there (further away). The trick
   is that the difference is only the first vowel, and it's the same pair everywhere in the
   language: *idu*/*adu* (this/that), *ivaru*/*avaru* (this person/that person),
   *illi*/*alli*. **i- is near, a- is away.** My own hook: "i" as in "in my hand", "a" as in
   "away". The app warned that getting it wrong says the exact opposite, and separately
   warned that *illa* (with an -a) is "no", not "here" — three near-identical words to keep
   apart.

2. **Why `snehitaru`, not `snehitagalu`** — because *-galu* is the plural ending for
   **things** and *-aru* is the plural ending for **people**. Friends are people. The test
   the app gave me: ask "is it a person?" — person → *-aru*, thing → *-galu*. So
   *pustakagalu*, *manegalu*, *hoovugalu* (all things), but *snehitaru*.

3. **`olleya` vs `chennagide`** — both translate "good" in English but they do different
   jobs and are not interchangeable. *Olleya* is only ever **in front of a noun**: *olleya
   oota* = "good food". It cannot end a sentence. *Chennagide* is for saying something **is**
   good, as the whole predicate: *oota chennagide* = "the food is good", *adu chennagide* =
   "that is good". English uses one word for both slots; Kannada doesn't. (*Chennagide* is
   visibly built from *chennagi-* + *ide*, the "it is" from lesson 3 — same *chennagi-* as
   in *chennagiddeeni*, "I am fine".)

4. **What `-ge` does on `manege`** — it marks the **destination**: "to". *Mane* = house,
   *manege* = to the house. It's an ending stuck onto the noun itself, never a separate word
   in front like English "to". Same idea, I was told, for any destination.

5. **Is `naanu` required in `naanu chennagiddeeni`?** — No. The *-eeni* on the end of the
   verb **already means "I"**, so the pronoun is redundant; *chennagiddeeni* on its own is a
   complete sentence and is what people actually say when answering. The general rule I was
   given: Kannada drops the pronoun whenever the verb ending has already told you who, which
   is most of the time. (The matching "you" ending is *-eera*, as in *iddeera*, so *neevu*
   is droppable too.) The one place a pronoun earns its keep is **contrast** — *Chennagiddeeni,
   neevu?* ("I'm fine, and you?"), where setting yourself against the other person is the
   whole point.

## Section 3: honest self-assessment

**What I can genuinely produce, unprompted, without thinking hard:**
about a dozen sentences, all short.
*Namaskara. Chennagiddeeni. Hegiddeera? Idu yenu? Ivaru yaaru? Mane yelli ide? Neeru beku.
Oota beda. Lekka kodi. Dhanyavada. Sari. Kshamisi.*
Plus reliable slot-filling in four frames: `[possessive] + [adjective] + noun`,
`[thing] beku/beda`, `[thing] yelli ide?`, `[number] + noun`. That's real, and it's more
than nothing.

**What I can only recognise, not produce:** *aatana*, *avara*, *ketta*, *kathe*, *avaru*,
*hoguttene* outside the one sentence I drilled it in. If you said them to me I'd know them;
I would not reach for them.

**What I've probably got wrong without knowing it:** everything about *nanna* vs *nimma* in
a live exchange — I was taught two contradictory things and I don't know which lesson lied.
And I suspect *naanu neeru beku* is wrong even though the app called it perfect.

**Could I survive a five-minute exchange with a Kannada speaker? No.** I could survive
about **forty seconds** of it — hello, I'm fine, how are you, what's your name, mine is X.
After that I'd collapse, and here's precisely why:

- I have **one verb**. *Hogu / hoguttene*. I cannot say eat, drink, come, have, see, know,
  understand, speak. I cannot say "I don't understand" or "please say that again", which are
  the two most important sentences a beginner can own and neither was taught.
- I have **no past or future**, and no "I am" for anything but "fine".
- I have **no way to say a person is somewhere** — I was explicitly told *ide* doesn't do it
  and never given the replacement.
- I can ask *yelli ide?* but I **could not understand the answer**, because I know two place
  words (*illi*, *alli*) and no directions, no "near", no "left/right", no street nouns.
- Same for *eshtu?* — I can ask how much, and I only count to five, so any real price is
  noise to me.
- Everything I know is **written transliteration read off a screen at my own pace**. I have
  never had to parse this at speaking speed. *Nanna* and *nimma* were called "near-twins on
  the page" by the app itself — at conversational speed I'd have no chance, and the same
  goes for *illi/alli/illa*.

Honest summary: after ten lessons I have a **decent tourist phrasebook and three or four
genuine grammar rules I could defend** (question word placement, number-vs-plural,
people-vs-things plurals, *olleya* vs *chennagide*, the dropped pronoun). What I do **not**
have is a language. The grammar I was given is unusually good for a beginner course — those
rules are stated as rules, contrasted, and reinforced across lessons, which is why they
survived to the exam. The verb inventory is the hole, and it's a big one: one verb in ten
lessons means every sentence I can make is a naming, wanting, or pointing sentence. Nothing
happens in my Kannada.
