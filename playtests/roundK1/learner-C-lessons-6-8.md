# Learner C — Kannada lessons 6, 7, 8

Adult beginner, native English speaker, no prior Indian-language study. Coming in off lessons 1–5 with about 25 words in my notebook and — this matters — **zero grammar**. Nobody has ever told me what order Kannada words go in, or how to say "is", or how to say "have". Keep that in mind for everything below.

---

## Lesson 6 — "The 'Who'"

### The vocabulary stretch (steps 1–5)

This part was fine. Five words, one at a time, with a pronunciation respelling under each:

> 🔊 Tutor: Hey there! I'm Miko, your friendly guide. 🐾 "Who" is **Yaaru**. Your turn — say **Yaaru**
>         say it like: yaa-ru

Then **Ivaru** ("this person"), **Snehita** (friend), **Shikshaka** (teacher), **Akka** (elder sister).

One thing I noticed straight away: the very first word gets a nice framing — *"Who" is **Yaaru***. Every word after it gets the flatter *To say "Friend", say **Snehita***. Small thing, but the first one reads like teaching and the rest read like a drill sergeant.

**First annoyance.** I typed `Aka` instead of `Akka` — a genuine slip, one dropped letter.

> 🔊 Tutor: Not quite — here it is again. To say "Elder sister", say **Akka**

Flat wrong. No "close — double the k". I'll come back to this, because lesson 7 treats the *identical* mistake completely differently.

### The memory check (steps 6–8)

Three questions, reverse order of teaching (Akka, Shikshaka, Snehita). I got all three. This bit works.

### Sentence building (steps 9–11) — where it fell apart

> 🔊 Tutor: Ask 'Who is this?'.

And I just sat there. **I have never been taught a single thing about Kannada word order.** Five lessons in and every prompt so far has been "say this one word". Now, with no transition, I'm supposed to assemble a sentence. So I did what a real person does and said out loud that I was guessing:

> me: `guessing here, you havent shown me word order yet - Ivaru yaaru?`
> 🔊 Tutor: You're right about the word order.
> 🔊 Tutor: Not quite. Hint: This person + who. Ask 'Who is this?'.

Read that again. It told me I was **right about the word order**, then told me I was **wrong**, then gave me a hint — "This person + who" — that describes *exactly the answer I had just given*. Three sentences, two of which contradict each other and one of which hands me back my own answer. I genuinely could not tell whether I'd got it right or not.

What had actually happened, I think, is that my English commentary was mixed in with my answer and the grader choked on it. So I retyped the bare words:

> me: `Ivaru yaaru`
> 👁 💡 *Yaaru* goes at the end of the question. *Ivaru* is the polite way to refer to someone present — safer than pointing.
> 🔊 Tutor: Perfect!

Correct. Same words. The only difference was that I hadn't talked. **The app punishes you for thinking out loud.** And the useful grammar note — "Yaaru goes at the end of the question" — only appears *after* you've already guessed right. That note should have come *before* the first sentence prompt. It's the single most useful sentence in the whole lesson and it's used as a reward instead of as teaching.

Next:

> 🔊 Tutor: Say 'This is my friend'.

I noticed there's no word for "is" anywhere in my notebook, and nobody has mentioned that Kannada drops it. I guessed `Ivaru nanna snehita` — literally "this-person my friend" — and it was accepted. So the copula just... doesn't exist, and I had to work that out by accident. Never stated.

### The thing that actually made me want to close the laptop

> 🔊 Tutor: Say 'She is my elder sister'.

I have no word for "she". I have `Avara = His/Her (formal)` from lesson 5, but that's a possessive, not a pronoun. So I did the honest thing:

> me: `you have not taught me the word for she`
> 🔊 Tutor: I would rather not answer that with a sentence you have not been taught yet — it will come. Let's stay with what we have covered.
> 🔊 Tutor: So — Say 'She is my elder sister'.

That is a **completely broken loop**. It refuses to teach me the word, on the grounds that I haven't been taught it — and then immediately demands I produce a sentence containing it. "Let's stay with what we have covered" and "say 'She is my elder sister'" cannot both be true.

I tried rephrasing as a plain question, which any human tutor would just answer:

> me: `what is the kannada word for she?`
> 🔊 Tutor: I would rather not answer that with a sentence you have not been taught yet — it will come. Let's stay with what we have covered.
> 🔊 Tutor: So — Say 'She is my elder sister'.

Word-for-word identical canned response. Twice. At this point I was properly irritated — I'm being stonewalled by a tutor for asking a vocabulary question, which is the most normal thing a learner can do.

I got out of it by using only words I *had* been given: `Ivaru nanna akka` ("this person is my elder sister"). Accepted, "Spot on!".

So the actual expected answer for **"She"** was **Ivaru** — the word I'd just been taught means **"this person"**. Which means:
1. The English prompt was wrong. It should have said "Say 'This is my elder sister'", or the lesson should have taught a real third-person pronoun.
2. The refusal was refusing to explain a word it had *already taught me*, thirty seconds earlier, in the same lesson.
3. If the answer was Ivaru all along, the "it will come" line is nonsense — it had already come.

That's the worst moment across all three lessons. A beginner who didn't happen to try substituting a synonym would be stuck there forever.

### Conversation (steps 12–15)

Four prompts, all fine, all recombinations of the same three patterns:

- "Someone points at a person. Ask who they are." → `Ivaru yaaru?` ✓
- "Tell Miko this person is your teacher." → `Ivaru nanna shikshaka` ✓
- "Introduce your friend to Miko." → `Ivaru nanna snehita` ✓
- "Ask who the teacher is." → `Shikshaka yaaru?` ✓

Honestly the last one felt like a freebie — it's the first pattern with one noun swapped. And "Introduce your friend to Miko" is a nicely open prompt that in practice has exactly one accepted answer, which I only knew because I'd typed it two steps earlier.

Ended with a summary card. Notably the card says **"Ivaru — This person (polite)"** — which is the definition that makes the "She is my elder sister" prompt indefensible.

## Lesson 7 — "Basic Numbers"

Easiest of the three by a distance. One through five: **Ondu, Eradu, Mooru, Naalku, Aidu**. No grammar, no ambiguity, nothing to misunderstand. This is the lesson that works.

**But here's the inconsistency that annoyed me.** In the memory check I mistyped `Nalku` (dropped an 'a'):

> 🔊 Tutor: Exactly! Note the spelling: **Naalku**. What's the word for "Three"?

Marked **correct**, with a gentle spelling nudge. That is exactly the right behaviour. It's also the exact opposite of what happened in lesson 6, where `Aka` for `Akka` got a flat "Not quite" and a full repeat. Same class of error — one missing letter — two completely different verdicts, in consecutive lessons. As a learner I now have no idea how precise I'm expected to be, and that uncertainty is genuinely worse than either rule applied consistently.

Sentence and conversation stages went clean:

- "Count from one to three." → `Ondu, eradu, mooru` ✓
- "Say 'two books'." → `Eradu pustaka` ✓ **← remember this one**
- "Say 'five houses'." → `Aidu mane` ✓ **← and this one**
- "Miko asks how many books you have. Say three." → `Mooru pustaka` ✓
- "Count all the way from one to five." ✓
- "Say 'four friends'." → `Naalku snehita` ✓
- "Ask for two waters." → `Eradu neeru beku` ✓

That last one was satisfying — first time the course let me build something out of an old lesson's vocabulary (`beku` from L4) plus a new one. More of that, please.

No grammar note anywhere about *why* "two books" is `Eradu pustaka` and not something pluralised. At the time I assumed Kannada simply doesn't mark plural after a number. Fair assumption. Hold that thought.

## Lesson 8 — "Plurals"

### The rule it teaches, and immediately breaks

> 🔊 Tutor: 🐾 **Galu** is the ending that makes a word plural. Your turn — say **Galu**

Clear. Absolute. *The* ending. Then:

- "Books" → **Pustakagalu** ✓ consistent
- "Friends" → **Snehitaru** ✗ **wait, what?**

`Snehita` + `-ru`. Not `-galu`. The rule it stated one step earlier, broken on the very next word, with zero acknowledgement. So I asked, exactly like a person would:

> me: `wait, why is it snehitaru and not snehitagalu? you said galu makes things plural`
> 👁 💡 Galu is the ending that makes a word plural.
> 🔊 Tutor: Here it is again. 🐾 To say "Friends", say **Snehitaru**

It answered my question by **repeating the very claim my question had disproved**, and then asked me again for the counterexample. That's not a tutor, that's a stuck record. The real answer — that Kannada uses `-ru` for people/animate nouns and `-galu` for things — is one sentence long, and the app has clearly got the data to know it, because it grades on it.

Then: "Houses" → **Manegalu** ✓, and:

> 🔊 Tutor: 🐾 To say "Flowers", say **Hoovugalu**

I've never seen "flower". It's teaching me the plural of a word I don't own. So I flagged it:

> me: `i dont know the word for flower, you never taught me hoovu`
> 🔊 Tutor: That's a great question! "Hoovu" means "flower" in Kannada. So when we add the plural ending "galu", it becomes "Hoovugalu" for "flowers".

**This is a genuinely good answer** and it's what I wanted throughout. But it makes the lesson 6 stonewall much worse in hindsight: the same app, asked the same shape of question ("you haven't taught me X"), sometimes explains warmly and sometimes fires a canned refusal. Two lessons apart. I can't predict which I'll get, so I stop asking — which is the opposite of what you want.

Also worth saying plainly: `Hoovugalu` is taught as vocabulary while `Hoovu` never is. At the end of this lesson I can say "flowers" but not "flower".

### The trap it set for me and then sprang

Memory check, third question:

> 🔊 Tutor: What's the word for "Friends"?
> me: `Snehitagalu`
> 🔊 Tutor: Not quite! What's the word for "Friends"?

I applied the rule the app itself taught me in step 1 of this same lesson, and got marked wrong with **no explanation of why**. Not "close — people take -ru". Just "Not quite!" and the question again. If I hadn't already been suspicious from four steps earlier I'd have had no route to the answer at all except guessing. `Snehitaru` was accepted on retry.

This is the clearest single design failure in the three lessons: the app teaches an over-simplified rule, then penalises you for following it, then declines to explain the exception when you ask directly.

### The contradiction with lesson 7

> 🔊 Tutor: Say 'three books'.
> me: `Mooru pustakagalu`
> 👁 💡 *pustaka* became *pustakagalu*. That -galu ending is what pluralises it.
> 🔊 Tutor: Perfect!

Yesterday's lesson accepted **`Eradu pustaka`** for "two books" as *Correct!*. Today's lesson wants **`Mooru pustakagalu`** for "three books" and explicitly praises the plural ending. Identical construction, number + noun, two different required answers, and **nothing anywhere acknowledges the change**. Same for `Aidu mane` (L7) vs `Eradu manegalu` (L8).

One of those two lessons is teaching me something wrong, and as a learner I have no way to tell which. I'd bet on lesson 7 being the wrong one, but I'm guessing, and I shouldn't have to.

### The refusal loop, again

> 🔊 Tutor: Tell Miko you have four friends.

No verb "have" anywhere in five lessons. So:

> me: `you havent taught me a word for have`
> 🔊 Tutor: I would rather not answer that with a sentence you have not been taught yet — it will come. Let's stay with what we have covered.
> 🔊 Tutor: So — Tell Miko you have four friends.

The identical canned refusal from lesson 6, in the identical shape: *"let's stay with what we have covered"* immediately followed by a demand for something we have explicitly not covered. Twice across three lessons, both times triggered by me doing the honest thing.

I guessed `Nanna naalku snehitaru` ("my four friends") → "Exactly!". So again, the English prompt promises a construction the Kannada answer doesn't contain. "You have four friends" is not what `Nanna naalku snehitaru` says.

Rest of the conversation stage was clean: `Aidu pustakagalu`, `Hoovugalu beku`, `Nanna pustakagalu`.

## The patterns, pulled together

**1. It demands sentences it never taught me to build.** Lessons 1–7 are pure vocabulary. There is no point at which anyone says "Kannada puts the question word last", or "there's no word for 'is'", or "possessive goes before the noun". I derived all three by guessing and getting lucky. The one time a word-order note appeared, it appeared *after* I'd already answered correctly.

**2. English prompts don't match the Kannada answers.** "She is my elder sister" → `Ivaru nanna akka` (this person). "Tell Miko you have four friends" → `Nanna naalku snehitaru` (my four friends). If I actually learned from the English side, I'd now believe `Ivaru` means "she" and `Nanna` means "I have".

**3. The "I don't know that" handling is a coin flip.** Lesson 8 explained `Hoovu` warmly and usefully. Lessons 6 and 8 both fired the same canned stonewall — including for a word (`Ivaru`) it had taught me minutes earlier. And the stonewall is self-contradictory on its face.

**4. Typo tolerance is inconsistent.** `Aka`→`Akka` failed hard (L6). `Nalku`→`Naalku` passed with a friendly note (L7). Pick one.

**5. Rules are taught as absolutes and then broken silently.** "Galu is *the* ending that makes a word plural" — then `Snehitaru`, then my rule-following answer marked wrong with no reason, then my direct question answered by re-reciting the broken rule.

**6. Lessons contradict each other and nothing notices.** `Eradu pustaka` (L7, correct) vs `Mooru pustakagalu` (L8, correct). Both can't be.

**7. Typing anything other than the bare answer breaks the grader.** `guessing here... Ivaru yaaru?` → wrong. `Ivaru yaaru` → perfect. Discourages exactly the behaviour a tutor should want.

**8. Minor, but:** the "You can now say" card for lesson 8 lists **"Galu — (Plural suffix)"** as something I can *say*. It's a suffix. I can't say it on its own.

## Honest self-assessment

### Kannada I could actually produce right now, unprompted, without looking

Words: `Yaaru`, `Ivaru`, `Snehita`, `Shikshaka`, `Akka`, `Ondu`, `Eradu`, `Mooru`, `Naalku`, `Aidu`, `Pustakagalu`, `Snehitaru`, `Manegalu`.

Sentence patterns — genuinely only three, and I could produce them cold:
- `Ivaru yaaru?` — who is this
- `Ivaru nanna [noun]` — this is my ___ (snehita / shikshaka / akka)
- `[number] [noun]` — four friends, five books

Counting 1–5 out loud, forwards. Not backwards, not reliably.

### Kannada I recognise but could not produce

- `Hoovugalu` — I'd know it if I saw it, but "flower" and "flowers" haven't separated in my head, and I have no idea if the singular is `Hoovu` or `Hovu`. Fifty-fifty on spelling it.
- `Galu` as a productive rule — I can attach it to `pustaka` and `mane` because I've *seen* those two done. I could not confidently pluralise a new word, because the one time I tried applying the rule myself (`Snehitagalu`) I was told I was wrong and never told why.
- `Naalku` — I can say it, I keep spelling it `Nalku`.

### Things I now believe that are probably wrong

- That `Ivaru` means "she". The app graded it that way.
- That number + noun sometimes takes a plural and sometimes doesn't, and it's arbitrary. That's what the L7/L8 contradiction taught me.
- That there is no way to say "is" or "have" in Kannada at all, rather than those just being things nobody has explained yet.

### Could I use any of this with a real person?

I could point at someone and ask `Ivaru yaaru?`, and I could answer `Ivaru nanna snehita`. That's a real, usable exchange and I'm pleased I have it. Everything else I'd need a menu and a pointing finger. Three lessons, fifteen new words, and about ninety seconds of usable conversation — the vocabulary is going in fine, but I'm not being taught how to *build* anything, and every time I asked for the piece I was missing I had a 50% chance of being told to stop asking.
